# Thermal Physics

> Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 — Topic 2. The kinetic particle model of matter, thermal expansion, temperature and specific heat capacity, changes of state, and the three methods of thermal energy transfer: conduction, convection and radiation.

## Notes

### States of matter and the kinetic particle model

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arrangement | regular, closely packed | irregular, close together | irregular, far apart |
| Motion | vibrate about fixed positions | move randomly, sliding past each other | move rapidly and randomly |
| Density | high | usually high | low |
| Compressible? | no | almost no | yes |
| Shape / volume | fixed shape and volume | fixed volume, takes container shape | fills the container |

The **kinetic particle model** explains all of this: matter is made of particles that are in constant random motion, and the temperature of a substance measures the average kinetic energy of its particles.

### Brownian motion

Smoke particles viewed through a microscope jitter about in random, jerky paths. This is **Brownian motion**, and it is evidence that the particles of a fluid are themselves moving randomly: the visible smoke particles are bombarded unevenly on different sides by fast, invisible air (or water) molecules.

Two points examiners look for:

- The motion is **random** — not caused by convection currents or by the smoke particles being "alive".
- It is **more vigorous at higher temperature**, because the fluid molecules have more kinetic energy.

### Gas pressure in terms of particles

The particles of a gas collide with the walls of their container; each collision exerts a tiny force, and the total force per unit area is the **pressure**.

- Reducing the volume at constant temperature crowds the particles together, so collisions are more frequent and the pressure rises.
- Raising the temperature makes the particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and with greater force, so the pressure rises.

### Thermal expansion

When a substance is heated, its particles vibrate (or move) more vigorously and, on average, push each other slightly further apart. The substance expands.

For the same temperature rise, **gases expand much more than liquids, and liquids expand slightly more than solids**. This is because the particles in a gas are only weakly held together, so a small increase in motion produces a large increase in separation.

Consequences and uses:

- Gaps must be left between lengths of railway track and in bridge expansion joints.
- A **bimetallic strip** (two metals with different expansion rates bonded together) bends when heated and is used as a switch in thermostats and fire alarms.
- Hot water in a glass jug can crack it if the inner surface expands before the outer one.

### Temperature and internal energy

**Internal energy** is the total kinetic energy (from motion) plus potential energy (from separation) of all the particles in a substance.

When you heat an object and its temperature rises, you have increased the **average kinetic energy** of its particles. Note that internal energy is not the same as temperature: a large block of metal at $50^\circ\mathrm{C}$ holds far more internal energy than a small nail at $50^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because it contains far more particles.

### Specific heat capacity

The **specific heat capacity** $c$ of a substance is the energy needed to raise the temperature of unit mass of it by one degree:

$$E=mc\Delta T$$

where $E$ is energy in joules, $m$ is mass in kg, $c$ is in $\mathrm{J/(kg\,^\circ C)}$ or $\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, and $\Delta T$ is the temperature rise.

Water has an unusually large specific heat capacity, about $4200\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,^\circ C)}$. This is why it is used as a coolant in car engines and in central heating, and why coastal areas have smaller temperature swings than inland areas.

To measure $c$ for a metal block: weigh the block, insert a heater and a thermometer, record the temperature rise $\Delta T$ over a timed interval, and measure the electrical energy supplied with a joulemeter (or with $E=VIt$ from an ammeter, voltmeter and stopwatch).

### Changes of state

| Change | Name | Energy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| solid → liquid | melting | absorbed |
| liquid → solid | freezing / solidification | released |
| liquid → gas | boiling | absorbed |
| gas → liquid | condensation | released |
| solid → gas | sublimation | absorbed |

Pure water melts at $0^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and boils at $100^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at atmospheric pressure.

The crucial point: during melting and boiling the temperature **does not rise** even though energy is being supplied. The energy goes into increasing the separation of the particles (raising their potential energy) and breaking the bonds that hold them together, not into raising their average kinetic energy.

### Evaporation versus boiling

**Evaporation** happens only at the surface, at any temperature, and is slow. Within a liquid the molecules have a range of speeds; the fastest ones near the surface can escape if they are moving upwards fast enough to overcome the attraction of the others. Because the **most energetic** molecules leave, the average kinetic energy of those left behind falls — so **evaporation causes cooling**.

The rate of evaporation increases when the temperature is higher, the surface area is larger, and air moves over the surface (carrying away the escaped molecules).

**Boiling** happens throughout the liquid, only at the boiling temperature, and is rapid — bubbles of vapour form inside the liquid.

### Conduction

**Conduction** is the transfer of thermal energy through a material without the material itself moving. In a metal, the free (delocalised) electrons gain kinetic energy where the metal is hot and diffuse to cooler regions, transferring energy quickly; lattice vibrations also pass energy along. This is why metals are good conductors and non-metals (wood, plastic, air) are poor ones.

Trapped air is a very poor conductor, which is why cavity wall insulation, loft insulation, fibreglass and wool are effective — they trap pockets of still air.

### Convection

**Convection** occurs in fluids (liquids and gases). When part of a fluid is heated it expands, becomes less dense, and rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place. The result is a **convection current** that carries thermal energy around the whole fluid.

Convection needs a fluid, so it cannot occur in a solid, and it cannot occur in a vacuum.

Applications: hot water tanks draw off hot water from the top; refrigerators have their freezing compartment at the top so cold, dense air sinks; ventilation and sea breezes are convection currents.

### Thermal radiation

**Thermal radiation** is infrared radiation. Unlike conduction and convection it needs **no medium** — it can travel through a vacuum, which is how the Sun's energy reaches the Earth.

Every object emits and absorbs thermal radiation. The rate depends on the surface:

- A **black, dull** surface is a good emitter and a good absorber, and a poor reflector.
- A **white, shiny** surface is a poor emitter and a poor absorber, and a good reflector.

Solar panels are black to absorb well; the inside of a vacuum flask is shiny to reduce emission; houses in hot climates are often painted white.

### Constant temperature and the balance of rates

For an object's temperature to stay constant, the **rate** at which it receives energy must equal the **rate** at which it loses it. If the input rate exceeds the output rate the temperature rises; if it is less, the temperature falls. This is why a heater on a low setting does not necessarily make a room colder than a high setting — it simply settles at a lower equilibrium temperature.

The Earth's temperature works the same way: incoming radiation from the Sun is balanced by radiation emitted back into space. Changes to the atmosphere that reduce the outgoing rate cause the equilibrium temperature to rise.

## Examples

### Example 1 (Specific heat capacity)

An electric kettle contains $1.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Calculate the energy needed to bring the water to boiling point ($100^\circ\mathrm{C}$). Take $c=4200\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,^\circ C)}$.

**Solution**

$\Delta T=100-20=80^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

$$E=mc\Delta T=1.5\times 4200\times 80=504\,000\ \mathrm{J}$$

So about $5.0\times 10^5\ \mathrm{J}$ (or $504\ \mathrm{kJ}$) is needed, ignoring the energy needed to heat the kettle itself and any losses.

### Example 2 (Gas pressure in terms of particles)

A sealed flask of air is heated from $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $70^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Explain, in terms of particles, why the pressure in the flask increases.

**Solution**

Heating gives the air molecules more kinetic energy, so they move faster. Faster molecules collide with the walls of the flask **more often** and **with a larger force** each time. Pressure is the force per unit area on the walls, so more frequent and more forceful collisions produce a higher pressure. The volume is fixed and the mass of gas is fixed, so the increase comes entirely from the higher molecular speed.

### Example 3 (Choosing a surface)

A satellite in space must both avoid overheating in sunlight and avoid losing too much energy when in the Earth's shadow. Explain why engineers use shiny, mirror-like surfaces and reflective foil blankets.

**Solution**

A satellite is in a vacuum, so conduction and convection are impossible and radiation is the only way it can gain or lose energy. A shiny, mirror-like surface is a poor absorber, so it takes in little of the Sun's intense radiation; it is also a poor emitter, so it loses little of its own thermal energy when on the dark side. A black surface would absorb strongly in sunlight (overheating) and emit strongly in shadow (overcooling), so a shiny surface keeps the temperature far more stable.

## Exercises

**Q1.** Calculate the energy needed to raise the temperature of $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water from $15^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $100^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Use $c=4200\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,^\circ C)}$.

**A1.** $\Delta T=85^\circ\mathrm{C}$. $E=mc\Delta T=2.0\times 4200\times 85=714\,000\ \mathrm{J}\approx 7.1\times 10^5\ \mathrm{J}$.

**Q2.** A $0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ block of aluminium is supplied with $9000\ \mathrm{J}$ of energy and its temperature rises by $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium.

**A2.** $c=\dfrac{E}{m\Delta T}=\dfrac{9000}{0.50\times 20}=\dfrac{9000}{10}=900\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,^\circ C)}$.

**Q3.** State the difference between boiling and evaporation.

**A3.** Boiling occurs throughout the liquid, only at the boiling temperature, and is rapid, with bubbles of vapour forming inside the liquid. Evaporation occurs only at the surface, at any temperature, and is slow, with the most energetic molecules escaping.

**Q4.** Explain why evaporation of sweat cools the body.

**A4.** The molecules with the greatest kinetic energy escape from the surface of the sweat. The molecules left behind therefore have a lower average kinetic energy, so the liquid (and the skin beneath it) is cooler.

**Q5.** Explain, in terms of density, how a convection current forms in a beaker of water heated at the bottom.

**A5.** Water at the bottom is heated, expands, and becomes less dense than the surrounding water. It rises, and cooler, denser water sinks to replace it. The resulting circulation — rising warm water and sinking cool water — is the convection current.

**Q6.** Why does a metal saucepan usually have a wooden or plastic handle?

**A6.** Metal is a good conductor of thermal energy, so the body of the pan heats quickly and transfers energy to the food. Wood and plastic are poor conductors (insulators), so the handle stays cool enough to hold safely.

**Q7.** A householder wants to reduce heat loss through the roof. Suggest one method and explain which transfer process it reduces.

**A7.** Lay loft insulation (such as fibreglass or mineral wool) between the ceiling joists. It traps pockets of still air; air is a very poor conductor, so conduction through the roof space is greatly reduced. The trapped air also cannot circulate, so convection is reduced as well.

**Q8.** Explain why a black car parked in sunshine becomes hotter than a white car parked beside it.

**A8.** A black, dark surface is a good absorber of thermal (infrared) radiation, so it takes in a large fraction of the Sun's radiation. A white, shiny surface reflects most of that radiation and absorbs little. Since the black car absorbs energy at a higher rate, its temperature rises higher before the rate of energy loss balances the rate of gain.

**Q9.** A sealed syringe contains air at room temperature. The plunger is pushed in slowly, halving the volume, while the temperature is kept constant. Explain, in terms of particles, what happens to the pressure.

**A9.** The same number of molecules now occupy half the volume, so they are closer together and collide with the walls twice as often per second. More frequent collisions produce a larger force per unit area, so the pressure doubles.
