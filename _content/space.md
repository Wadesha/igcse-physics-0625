## Notes

### The Earth and the Moon

The **Earth** is a planet. It:

- **rotates** on its own axis once approximately every 24 hours, which produces day and night;
- **orbits** the Sun once approximately every 365 days (one year).

The Earth's axis is tilted, and combined with the yearly orbit this tilt produces the **seasons**: when a hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun it receives more direct radiation and has longer days, so it is summer there.

The **Moon** is a natural satellite. It orbits the Earth approximately once every month (about 27.3 days for one orbit; about 29.5 days between identical phases). The changing relative positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon produce the **phases of the Moon**, and the alignment of all three produces **eclipses**.

### The Solar System

The Solar System consists of:

- one **star**, the Sun, at the centre, containing most of the mass of the Solar System;
- eight **planets** orbiting the Sun, in order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune;
- **minor planets** — dwarf planets such as Pluto, and asteroids, mostly in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter;
- **comets** — lumps of ice and dust in highly elliptical orbits;
- **moons** (natural satellites) orbiting the planets.

The four inner planets are small and rocky, while the four outer planets are much larger and are gas giants. Jupiter is by far the largest planet.

### Gravitational field strength and orbits

Every mass attracts every other mass. The **gravitational field strength** $g$ at a point is the force per unit mass placed there; it depends on the mass of the body producing the field and on the distance from it — a more massive planet has a stronger field, and the field gets weaker further away.

The gravitational force is what keeps a planet in orbit around the Sun and a moon in orbit around a planet. The force acts towards the centre of the orbit, continuously changing the direction of the orbiting body's velocity, so the orbit is a continuously "falling" path.

Orbits are **elliptical**, not perfectly circular. In an elliptical orbit the speed is not constant: an object moves **faster when it is closer** to the body it orbits, because the gravitational force (and therefore the acceleration) is greater there. This is why comets race through the inner Solar System and crawl at the outer edge of their orbits.

### Orbital speed

For an approximately circular orbit of radius $r$, the distance travelled in one orbit is the circumference $2\pi r$, and the time taken is the period $T$, so the average orbital speed is

$$v=\frac{2\pi r}{T}$$

This works for any orbiting body — a planet round the Sun, the Moon round the Earth, or a satellite round the Earth — provided you use the radius of the orbit, not the radius of the body.

### The Sun and other stars

The **Sun** is a star of medium size. It is powered by **nuclear fusion**, in which hydrogen nuclei join to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy. The Sun contains most of the mass of the Solar System, which is why everything else orbits it.

A **light-year** is the distance light travels in one year:

$$1\ \text{light-year}=9.5\times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{m}$$

Light travels at $3.0\times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in a vacuum, so light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach the Earth, while light from the next nearest star takes over four years. Looking out into space is therefore looking back in time.

### The life cycle of a star

A star begins as a cloud of dust and gas called a **nebula**. Gravity pulls the material together; as it collapses it heats up, and when the core becomes hot and dense enough, nuclear fusion begins and a **star** is born. The star then spends most of its life as a **main sequence** star, in which the inward pull of gravity is balanced by the outward pressure from the energy released by fusion — a stable balance that lasts billions of years.

What happens next depends on the mass of the star:

- A star like the Sun eventually expands into a **red giant**, then sheds its outer layers as a **planetary nebula**, leaving a small, hot, dense **white dwarf** behind, which slowly cools and fades.
- A much more massive star becomes a **red supergiant**, then explodes as a **supernova**. The remnant is either an extremely dense **neutron star** or, if massive enough, a **black hole**, whose gravity is so strong that not even light can escape.

### Galaxies and the Universe

The **Milky Way** is a galaxy — a huge collection of many billions of stars held together by gravity — and our Sun is one of those stars. The Milky Way is one of many billions of galaxies in the Universe.

### Redshift and the expanding Universe

When a source of light moves away from an observer, the observed wavelength of its light is increased — the light is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum. This is **redshift**.

Observations show that the light from distant galaxies is redshifted, and that the redshift is **greater for more distant galaxies**. The conclusion is that distant galaxies are moving away from us, and that the further away they are, the faster they are receding: the **Universe is expanding**.

Note that it is the space between galaxies that is expanding; the galaxies themselves are being carried apart rather than flying through space from a single point.

### Hubble's law

The speed of recession $v$ of a galaxy is proportional to its distance $d$ from us:

$$v=H_0 d$$

$H_0$ is the **Hubble constant**, defined as the ratio of the speed of recession to the distance. Its current estimate is about $2.2\times 10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Because $\dfrac{1}{H_0}$ has the units of time, it gives a rough estimate of the age of the Universe.

In practice the distance of a far galaxy is estimated from its redshift, and then Hubble's law is used to infer how fast it is receding.

### The cosmic microwave background radiation

Astronomers detect low-intensity microwave radiation arriving almost uniformly from **all directions** in the sky. This is the **cosmic microwave background radiation** (CMBR).

Its existence is strong evidence for the **Big Bang** model. The Universe began extremely hot and dense; as it expanded it cooled, and the radiation produced shortly after the Big Bang has been stretched to microwave wavelengths by the expansion of space itself. It is, in effect, the afterglow of the early Universe, and its presence is very hard to explain in any steady-state model.

## Examples

### Example 1 (Orbital speed)

A satellite orbits the Earth at a height such that its orbital radius is $7.0\times 10^6\ \mathrm{m}$, and it completes one orbit in $5.8\times 10^3\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate its average orbital speed.

**Solution**

$$v=\frac{2\pi r}{T}=\frac{2\pi\times 7.0\times 10^6}{5.8\times 10^3}=\frac{4.40\times 10^7}{5.8\times 10^3}\approx 7.6\times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

So the satellite travels at about $7.6\ \mathrm{km/s}$.

### Example 2 (Light travel time)

The Sun is about $1.5\times 10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}$ from the Earth. Calculate how long light takes to travel from the Sun to the Earth, using $c=3.0\times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

**Solution**

$$t=\frac{d}{c}=\frac{1.5\times 10^{11}}{3.0\times 10^8}=5.0\times 10^2=500\ \mathrm{s}$$

That is about $8.3$ minutes. So we see the Sun as it was about eight minutes ago.

### Example 3 (Hubble's law)

A galaxy is found to be receding at $4.4\times 10^4\ \mathrm{km/s}$. Using $H_0=2.2\times 10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, estimate its distance from the Earth in metres.

**Solution**

First convert the speed to metres per second: $v=4.4\times 10^4\ \mathrm{km/s}=4.4\times 10^7\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

$$d=\frac{v}{H_0}=\frac{4.4\times 10^7}{2.2\times 10^{-18}}=2.0\times 10^{25}\ \mathrm{m}$$

So the galaxy is about $2.0\times 10^{25}\ \mathrm{m}$ away. (In light-years this is $\dfrac{2.0\times 10^{25}}{9.5\times 10^{15}}\approx 2.1\times 10^9$ light-years.)

## Exercises

**Q1.** State the order of the eight planets, starting with the one closest to the Sun.

**A1.** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

**Q2.** Explain the difference between the Earth rotating on its axis and the Earth orbiting the Sun, and state what each causes.

**A2.** Rotation is the Earth spinning on its own axis, once about every 24 hours, and it causes day and night. Orbiting (revolution) is the Earth's motion around the Sun, once about every year; combined with the tilt of the Earth's axis it causes the seasons.

**Q3.** The Moon orbits the Earth with a period of about $27.3$ days at an orbital radius of $3.8\times 10^8\ \mathrm{m}$. Calculate its average orbital speed.

**A3.** $T=27.3\times 24\times 3600=2.36\times 10^6\ \mathrm{s}$. $v=\dfrac{2\pi r}{T}=\dfrac{2\pi\times 3.8\times 10^8}{2.36\times 10^6}=\dfrac{2.39\times 10^9}{2.36\times 10^6}\approx 1.0\times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (about $1.0\ \mathrm{km/s}$).

**Q4.** Explain why a comet travels fastest when it is closest to the Sun.

**A4.** The Sun's gravitational pull on the comet is strongest when the comet is closest to it. This larger force produces a larger acceleration, and over the part of the orbit near the Sun the force partly acts along the direction of motion, increasing the comet's speed. Energy is conserved: as the comet falls closer to the Sun it loses gravitational potential energy and gains kinetic energy.

**Q5.** Calculate the distance, in metres, corresponding to $4.2$ light-years.

**A5.** $4.2\times 9.5\times 10^{15}=4.0\times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{m}$.

**Q6.** State what is meant by the main sequence stage of a star's life, and explain why the star is stable during this stage.

**A6.** The main sequence is the long stage during which a star is fusing hydrogen into helium in its core. The star is stable because the inward force of gravity pulling its material together is balanced by the outward pressure produced by the energy released in fusion, so its size and temperature stay roughly constant for billions of years.

**Q7.** Describe how a star much more massive than the Sun ends its life, naming the stages.

**A7.** It expands into a red supergiant, then explodes as a supernova. The remnant left behind is a neutron star, or, if enough mass remains, a black hole from which light cannot escape.

**Q8.** Explain what redshift is and how it provides evidence that the Universe is expanding.

**A8.** Redshift is an increase in the observed wavelength of light from a source that is moving away from the observer, shifting its spectrum towards the red end. Light from distant galaxies is redshifted, and the more distant the galaxy the greater the redshift, which means more distant galaxies are receding faster. Since essentially all distant galaxies are receding from us, the Universe as a whole is expanding.

**Q9.** A galaxy is $3.0\times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{m}$ away. Using $H_0=2.2\times 10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, calculate the speed at which it is receding.

**A9.** $v=H_0 d=2.2\times 10^{-18}\times 3.0\times 10^{24}=6.6\times 10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (about $6600\ \mathrm{km/s}$).

**Q10.** State what the cosmic microwave background radiation is and why it supports the Big Bang model.

**A10.** The CMBR is low-intensity microwave radiation detected almost uniformly from every direction in the sky. It is interpreted as radiation produced when the Universe was extremely hot and dense, shortly after the Big Bang, which has been stretched to microwave wavelengths by the subsequent expansion of space. Its existence — and its near-perfect uniformity — is a direct prediction of the Big Bang model and is difficult to explain in any model without a hot, dense early stage.
