## Notes

### Magnetism

Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. **Induced magnetism** is the temporary magnetism produced in a magnetic material when it is placed near a magnet — this is why an unmagnetised nail is attracted to either pole of a magnet.

- **Hard** magnetic materials (steel) keep their magnetism and make permanent magnets.
- **Soft** magnetic materials (iron) magnetise and demagnetise easily and are used in transformers and electromagnet cores.

A **magnetic field** is a region where a magnetic pole experiences a force. Its direction at any point is the direction of the force on a north pole placed there. Field lines run from north to south; where they are close together the field is strong, and field lines never cross. You can plot them with a small plotting compass.

### Electrostatics and electric fields

There are two kinds of charge, positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charge is measured in **coulombs** (C).

Charging two solids by friction (for example a plastic rod rubbed with a cloth) works by **transferring electrons**: the material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged, and the one that loses them becomes equally positively charged. Charge is conserved overall.

- **Conductors** (metals, graphite) contain free electrons that can move through the material.
- **Insulators** (plastics, glass) have electrons that are bound to their atoms and cannot move freely.

An **electric field** is a region where a charge experiences a force. By convention its direction is the direction of the force on a **positive** charge, so field lines point away from positive charges and towards negative ones. A practical use of electrostatic charging is a paint sprayer, where the charged droplets repel each other to give a fine, even spray and are attracted to the earthed object.

### Electric current

Current is the rate of flow of charge:

$$I=\frac{Q}{t}\qquad\text{or}\qquad Q=It$$

Current is measured with an **ammeter** connected in **series**; the unit is the ampere (A), where $1\ \mathrm{A}=1\ \mathrm{C/s}$.

**Conventional current** flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal, even though the electrons in a metal actually drift in the opposite direction.

**Direct current (d.c.)** flows in one direction only, from a cell or battery. **Alternating current (a.c.)** reverses direction periodically, as in mains electricity.

### E.m.f. and potential difference

The **e.m.f.** of a source is the electrical work done by the source in driving unit charge round a complete circuit:

$$E=\frac{W}{Q}$$

The **potential difference** (p.d.) between two points is the work done (energy converted) per unit charge moving between those points:

$$V=\frac{W}{Q}$$

Both are measured in **volts**, where $1\ \mathrm{V}=1\ \mathrm{J/C}$. The distinction matters: e.m.f. describes energy **supplied** to the charges, while p.d. describes energy **converted** by a component. A voltmeter is connected in **parallel** across the component.

For sources in series the e.m.f.s add; note that cells connected in opposite directions subtract.

### Resistance

Resistance measures how difficult it is for current to pass through a component:

$$R=\frac{V}{I}\qquad\text{so}\qquad V=IR$$

The unit is the ohm ($\Omega$). To measure the resistance of a component, connect an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel, take several pairs of readings, and calculate $R=\dfrac{V}{I}$ for each.

The resistance of a wire increases when it is **longer**, **thinner**, **hotter**, or made of a material with a higher resistivity.

### Current–voltage characteristics

- **Metal wire at constant temperature:** a straight line through the origin — current is proportional to p.d., so the resistance is constant. This is **Ohm's law**.
- **Filament lamp:** the graph curves because the filament heats up and its resistance rises, so the current increases less than proportionally.
- **Semiconductor diode:** it conducts readily in one direction (forward bias) above about $0.6\ \mathrm{V}$, but almost not at all in the reverse direction.

### Electrical power and energy

Power is the rate of energy transfer:

$$P=VI$$

and the energy transferred in time $t$ is

$$E=VIt$$

For the mains supply, energy is billed in **kilowatt-hours** (kW h). One kilowatt-hour is the energy used by a $1\ \mathrm{kW}$ appliance running for one hour:

$$1\ \mathrm{kW\,h}=1000\ \mathrm{W}\times 3600\ \mathrm{s}=3.6\times 10^6\ \mathrm{J}$$

Cost = number of kW h × cost per kW h.

### Series circuits

In a series circuit there is only one path, so:

- the **current is the same at every point**;
- the e.m.f. is shared between the components, so the p.d.s add up to the supply e.m.f.;
- the combined resistance is the sum of the individual resistances: $R=R_1+R_2$.

### Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit each branch is connected directly across the supply, so:

- the **p.d. across each branch is the same**;
- the current from the source is the **sum** of the branch currents (the junction rule);
- the combined resistance is **less** than the smallest individual resistance: $\dfrac{1}{R}=\dfrac{1}{R_1}+\dfrac{1}{R_2}$.

Lamps are connected in parallel in domestic wiring so that each gets the full mains p.d. and can be switched independently — if one fails, the others stay lit.

### Potential dividers

Two resistors in series divide the supply p.d. between them in proportion to their resistances:

$$\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}$$

Using a **variable resistor** (potentiometer) as the divider gives an output p.d. that can be varied from zero up to the supply p.d. Replacing one resistor with a thermistor gives a temperature-sensitive output, and with an LDR gives a light-sensitive output — the basis of many sensor circuits.

### Electrical safety

A mains circuit has three wires: the **live** wire (at a high alternating p.d.), the **neutral** wire (completed circuit, near earth p.d.) and the **earth** wire (a safety connection to the ground).

Main hazards:

- **Damaged insulation** — the live wire may be touched, or may short to earth.
- **Overheating cables** — too large a current in too thin a cable can start a fire.
- **Damp conditions** — water conducts, so it greatly increases the risk of shock.

Protection devices:

- A **fuse** is a thin wire that melts when the current exceeds its rating, breaking the circuit. It is fitted in the **live** wire.
- A **trip switch** (circuit breaker) opens automatically when the current exceeds a set value, and can be reset.
- **Earthing** connects the metal case of an appliance to the ground, so that if the live wire touches the case a large current flows to earth and blows the fuse rather than making the case live.

### Electromagnetic induction

If a conductor moves across a magnetic field (or the field around it changes), an **e.m.f. is induced** across the conductor. If the circuit is complete, an induced current flows.

The magnitude of the induced e.m.f. increases when the conductor moves faster, the magnetic field is stronger, and the length of conductor in the field is greater (for example a coil with more turns). The direction of the induced current **opposes the change that caused it**, so more work is needed to produce more current.

### The a.c. generator

A coil rotating in a magnetic field has an e.m.f. induced in it. Because the coil cuts the field at a varying rate, the induced e.m.f. alternates: it is zero when the coil is at right angles to the field and maximum when it is parallel to the field. Slip rings and brushes keep the connection to the rotating coil while allowing the current to reverse, giving an alternating output that varies sinusoidally with time.

### Magnetic field of a current

Any current-carrying conductor has a magnetic field around it:

- Around a **straight wire** the field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire; reversing the current reverses the field direction.
- Around a **solenoid** (a long coil) the field is like that of a bar magnet, with a north and a south end.

The field is stronger with a larger current, more turns per unit length, and (in a solenoid) an iron core. This is the basis of the **electromagnet**.

### The motor effect

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force, provided the conductor is not parallel to the field. The force is greatest when the conductor is perpendicular to the field and **zero** when it is parallel to it.

The three directions are mutually perpendicular and are given by **Fleming's left-hand rule**:

- **F**irst finger — direction of the magnetic **F**ield (north to south)
- **S**econd finger — direction of the **C**urrent
- **T**humb — direction of the **T**hrust (force, i.e. motion)

Reversing either the current or the field reverses the force; reversing both leaves it unchanged.

### The d.c. motor

A rectangular coil carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a turning effect because the force on one side of the coil is upwards while the force on the other side is downwards. A **commutator** (a split ring) reverses the current each half-turn, so the turning effect always acts in the same direction. The turning effect is increased by more turns, a larger current and a stronger field.

### Transformers

A transformer consists of a primary coil and a secondary coil wound on a soft iron core. An alternating current in the primary produces a changing magnetic field, which is carried by the core to the secondary, where it induces an alternating e.m.f. Transformers only work with a.c.

$$\frac{V_p}{V_s}=\frac{N_p}{N_s}$$

A **step-up** transformer has more turns on the secondary and increases the voltage; a **step-down** transformer has fewer turns and decreases it.

For high-voltage transmission, electricity is stepped up to a very high voltage and correspondingly low current. Because the power lost as heat in the cables is $I^2R$, reducing the current dramatically reduces the losses.

Assuming 100% efficiency, the input power equals the output power:

$$V_p I_p=V_s I_s$$

## Examples

### Example 1 (Current, charge and energy in a circuit)

A current of $2.5\ \mathrm{A}$ flows through a lamp for $4.0$ minutes. Calculate (a) the charge that passes through the lamp and (b) the energy transferred if the p.d. across the lamp is $12\ \mathrm{V}$.

**Solution**

(a) $t=4.0\times 60=240\ \mathrm{s}$, so

$$Q=It=2.5\times 240=600\ \mathrm{C}$$

(b) $E=VIt=12\times 2.5\times 240=7200\ \mathrm{J}$ (or $E=VQ=12\times 600=7200\ \mathrm{J}$).

### Example 2 (Series and parallel resistors)

Two resistors, $4.0\ \Omega$ and $6.0\ \Omega$, are connected (a) in series and (b) in parallel to a $12\ \mathrm{V}$ supply. Calculate the combined resistance and the supply current in each case.

**Solution**

(a) In series: $R=4.0+6.0=10.0\ \Omega$. The current is

$$I=\frac{V}{R}=\frac{12}{10.0}=1.2\ \mathrm{A}$$

(b) In parallel:

$$\frac{1}{R}=\frac{1}{4.0}+\frac{1}{6.0}=0.250+0.167=0.417$$

so $R=\dfrac{1}{0.417}=2.4\ \Omega$. The current is

$$I=\frac{12}{2.4}=5.0\ \mathrm{A}$$

Note that the parallel combination has a smaller resistance and draws a larger current.

### Example 3 (A step-down transformer)

A transformer has $1200$ turns on the primary and $60$ turns on the secondary, and is connected to a $240\ \mathrm{V}$ a.c. supply. Calculate the secondary voltage. If the primary current is $0.50\ \mathrm{A}$, calculate the secondary current, assuming 100% efficiency.

**Solution**

$$\frac{V_p}{V_s}=\frac{N_p}{N_s}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad V_s=V_p\times\frac{N_s}{N_p}=240\times\frac{60}{1200}=240\times 0.05=12\ \mathrm{V}$$

For 100% efficiency, $V_p I_p=V_s I_s$:

$$240\times 0.50=12\times I_s\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad I_s=\frac{120}{12}=10\ \mathrm{A}$$

The voltage is stepped down by a factor of 20 and the current is stepped up by the same factor.

## Exercises

**Q1.** A charge of $90\ \mathrm{C}$ passes a point in a circuit in $30\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate the current.

**A1.** $I=\dfrac{Q}{t}=\dfrac{90}{30}=3.0\ \mathrm{A}$.

**Q2.** A resistor has a p.d. of $9.0\ \mathrm{V}$ across it when the current through it is $0.30\ \mathrm{A}$. Calculate its resistance.

**A2.** $R=\dfrac{V}{I}=\dfrac{9.0}{0.30}=30\ \Omega$.

**Q3.** Calculate the power of a heater that operates from a $230\ \mathrm{V}$ supply with a current of $4.0\ \mathrm{A}$.

**A3.** $P=VI=230\times 4.0=920\ \mathrm{W}$.

**Q4.** A $2.0\ \mathrm{kW}$ heater runs for $3.0$ hours. Calculate the energy used in kW h and the cost at $0.15$ currency units per kW h.

**A4.** Energy $=2.0\times 3.0=6.0\ \mathrm{kW\,h}$. Cost $=6.0\times 0.15=0.90$ currency units.

**Q5.** Three resistors of $2.0\ \Omega$, $3.0\ \Omega$ and $5.0\ \Omega$ are connected in series. Calculate the total resistance.

**A5.** $R=2.0+3.0+5.0=10.0\ \Omega$.

**Q6.** A $6.0\ \Omega$ resistor and a $3.0\ \Omega$ resistor are connected in parallel. Calculate the combined resistance.

**A6.** $\dfrac{1}{R}=\dfrac{1}{6.0}+\dfrac{1}{3.0}=0.167+0.333=0.500$, so $R=\dfrac{1}{0.500}=2.0\ \Omega$.

**Q7.** Two resistors, $100\ \Omega$ and $300\ \Omega$, are connected in series across a $12\ \mathrm{V}$ supply. Calculate the p.d. across each resistor.

**A7.** Total resistance $=400\ \Omega$; current $=\dfrac{12}{400}=0.030\ \mathrm{A}$. Across the $100\ \Omega$ resistor: $V=IR=0.030\times 100=3.0\ \mathrm{V}$. Across the $300\ \Omega$ resistor: $V=0.030\times 300=9.0\ \mathrm{V}$. (Check: $3.0+9.0=12\ \mathrm{V}$, and the p.d.s are in the ratio of the resistances, $1:3$.)

**Q8.** Explain why a fuse must be connected in the live wire rather than the neutral wire.

**A8.** The fuse must break the connection to the high-voltage live wire. If it were in the neutral wire it would still stop the current, but the appliance's internal wiring would remain connected to the live wire at mains voltage, so the appliance would still be dangerous to touch even though it had stopped working.

**Q9.** State two reasons why electricity is transmitted at very high voltage.

**A9.** For a given power, a higher voltage means a smaller current ($P=VI$). The power lost as heat in the cables is $I^2R$, so reducing the current greatly reduces the energy lost; it also allows thinner, cheaper cables to be used.

**Q10.** A transformer has $500$ turns on the primary and $2000$ turns on the secondary, connected to a $50\ \mathrm{V}$ a.c. supply. State whether it is step-up or step-down and calculate the output voltage.

**A10.** There are more turns on the secondary, so it is a **step-up** transformer. $V_s=50\times\dfrac{2000}{500}=200\ \mathrm{V}$.

**Q11.** A wire is moved downwards through a horizontal magnetic field directed from left to right. State two changes that would increase the induced e.m.f.

**A11.** Move the wire faster, and use a stronger magnetic field. (Using a longer wire, or a coil with more turns, in the field would also increase it.)
