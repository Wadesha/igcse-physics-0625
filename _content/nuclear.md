## Notes

### The nuclear model of the atom

An atom consists of a tiny, dense, positively charged **nucleus** containing most of the mass, surrounded by **electrons** in orbit. The atom as a whole is electrically neutral because the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Position |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| proton | $+1$ | $1$ | in the nucleus |
| neutron | $0$ | $1$ | in the nucleus |
| electron | $-1$ | about $\dfrac{1}{1840}$ | orbiting the nucleus |

An atom becomes an **ion** when it loses or gains electrons: losing electrons leaves a positive ion, gaining electrons gives a negative ion. Ionisation never changes the element, because the number of protons is unchanged.

### The alpha particle scattering experiment

In the Geiger–Marsden experiment, alpha particles were fired at a very thin gold foil. The observations were:

1. **Most** alpha particles passed straight through with no deflection.
2. **A few** were deflected through small angles.
3. **A very small number** (about 1 in 8000) bounced back through more than $90^\circ$.

The conclusions:

- Most of the atom is **empty space** (most particles are undeflected).
- The atom contains a **small, dense, positively charged nucleus** — positive, because it repels the positive alpha particles, and dense because a few are turned right back.
- The electrons orbit far outside the nucleus.

This experiment overturned the earlier "plum pudding" model, in which the positive charge was spread throughout the atom.

### Nuclide notation and isotopes

The **proton number** $Z$ is the number of protons; it identifies the element. The **nucleon number** $A$ is the total number of protons plus neutrons. Therefore:

$$\text{number of neutrons}=A-Z$$

A nuclide is written as ${}^{A}_{Z}X$, where $X$ is the chemical symbol. For example, ${}^{235}_{\ 92}\mathrm{U}$ has 92 protons and $235-92=143$ neutrons.

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same $Z$) with different numbers of neutrons (different $A$). They have identical chemical properties, because chemistry depends on the electrons, but their nuclei differ in stability — some isotopes are stable, others radioactive.

### Nuclear fission and fusion

**Fission** is the splitting of a large, unstable nucleus (such as uranium-235) into two smaller nuclei plus neutrons, releasing energy. If the released neutrons go on to split further nuclei, a **chain reaction** results; this is controlled in a nuclear power station.

**Fusion** is the joining of two small nuclei (such as isotopes of hydrogen) to form a larger nucleus, releasing energy. Fusion is the process that powers the Sun and other stars, and it requires extremely high temperatures and pressures.

### Background radiation

**Background radiation** is the low-level ionising radiation that is always present. Significant sources include:

- **radon gas** in the air (from rocks and soil) — usually the largest natural contribution;
- rocks and soil, and building materials made from them;
- **cosmic rays** from space (more at high altitude);
- food and drink (naturally occurring radioactive isotopes);
- medical and industrial uses (a small artificial contribution).

Because background radiation is always present, any measurement of a source's activity must be **corrected**: count the background separately, then subtract it from the count measured with the source present.

$$\text{corrected count rate}=\text{measured count rate}-\text{background count rate}$$

Counts should be taken over a reasonably long time and averaged, because radioactive decay is random.

### Detecting radiation

- **Photographic film** — radiation darkens the film; film badges are worn by workers to monitor their dose.
- **Geiger–Müller (GM) tube** with a counter — each detected particle produces a pulse; the counter gives a count rate in counts per second or per minute. A GM tube detects alpha particles only if they can enter the tube, so it has a thin mica window.

### Alpha, beta and gamma radiation

| Radiation | Nature | Charge | Penetration | Ionising power | Deflected by fields? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| alpha $\alpha$ | helium nucleus, 2 protons + 2 neutrons | $+2$ | stopped by paper or a few cm of air | strong | yes, slightly |
| beta $\beta$ | fast electron from the nucleus | $-1$ | stopped by a few mm of aluminium | moderate | yes, strongly |
| gamma $\gamma$ | electromagnetic wave | $0$ | reduced by several cm of lead or metres of concrete | weak | no |

The pattern is that **the more strongly ionising a radiation is, the less penetrating it is**, because it loses its energy more quickly as it interacts with the atoms of the material.

In electric and magnetic fields, alpha and beta are deflected in **opposite directions** because their charges are opposite, and beta is deflected much more because an electron's mass is far smaller than an alpha particle's. Gamma is not deflected because it is uncharged.

### Radioactive decay and decay equations

Radioactive decay is a change in an unstable nucleus; it is random and spontaneous, and it is unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemical combination. When a nucleus emits alpha or beta radiation it becomes a nucleus of a **different element**.

The rules for balancing a decay equation:

- **Alpha decay:** $A$ decreases by 4 and $Z$ decreases by 2. An alpha particle is ${}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$.
- **Beta decay:** $A$ is unchanged and $Z$ increases by 1 (a neutron turns into a proton plus an electron, and the electron is ejected). Write the beta particle as ${}^{\ 0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$.
- **Gamma emission:** neither $A$ nor $Z$ changes; the nucleus simply loses surplus energy.

Two examples:

$${}^{238}_{\ 92}\mathrm{U}\ \rightarrow\ {}^{234}_{\ 90}\mathrm{Th}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$$

$${}^{14}_{\ 6}\mathrm{C}\ \rightarrow\ {}^{14}_{\ 7}\mathrm{N}+{}^{\ 0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$$

In every equation the total nucleon number and the total proton number must be the same on both sides.

### Half-life

The **half-life** of an isotope is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei (and hence the count rate) to fall to half its initial value.

Half-life is **constant** for a given isotope — it does not depend on how many nuclei are present, and it cannot be changed. After $n$ half-lives the fraction remaining is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$.

To find the half-life from a decay curve, choose a starting count rate, halve it, read off the time at which the curve reaches that halved value, and subtract the starting time.

### Choosing a source and working safely

The choice of source depends on the job:

- A **tracer** in the body should be a gamma emitter (so it can escape the body to be detected) with a half-life long enough to do the scan but short enough to decay quickly afterwards.
- A **thickness gauge** for paper uses a beta source, because the paper partially absorbs beta and the change in count rate reveals the thickness.
- A **smoke detector** uses an alpha source, because alpha ionises the air strongly and smoke absorbs the alpha particles, changing the current.

Ionising radiation can damage living cells, causing mutations, cell death, radiation burns and an increased risk of cancer. Safety precautions:

- keep the source as **far away** as possible and use handling tools (tongs);
- minimise the **time** of exposure;
- use **shielding** (lead screens, or perspex for beta);
- store sources in a clearly labelled, lead-lined container when not in use;
- wear a film badge to monitor the accumulated dose;
- never point a source at anyone, and never touch or ingest it.

## Examples

### Example 1 (Half-life from a decay curve)

A radioactive source has an initial count rate of $800$ counts/minute. After $12$ hours the count rate is $100$ counts/minute. Background radiation is $20$ counts/minute. Calculate the half-life of the source.

**Solution**

First correct for background:

- initial corrected count rate $=800-20=780$ counts/minute;
- final corrected count rate $=100-20=80$ counts/minute.

Now find how many halvings take $780$ down to $80$: $780\to 390\to 195\to 97.5\to 48.75$. After three half-lives the count rate is $97.5$, and after four it is $48.75$; $80$ lies between these, close to three half-lives.

A cleaner approach: $780\times\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}=80$, so $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}=0.103$, giving $n\approx 3.3$ half-lives in $12$ hours.

$$\text{half-life}\approx\frac{12}{3.3}\approx 3.6\ \text{hours}$$

### Example 2 (Balancing a decay equation)

Radon-222 decays by alpha emission. Write the balanced decay equation and identify the daughter nuclide.

**Solution**

Radon has $Z=86$. In alpha decay $A$ falls by 4 and $Z$ falls by 2:

$${}^{222}_{\ 86}\mathrm{Rn}\ \rightarrow\ {}^{218}_{\ 84}\mathrm{Po}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$$

Check: $222=218+4$ and $86=84+2$. The daughter nuclide has $Z=84$, which is polonium, so it is polonium-218.

### Example 3 (Interpreting the scattering experiment)

In the alpha particle scattering experiment, most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil but a very small number were deflected through more than $90^\circ$. Explain what each observation shows.

**Solution**

Most passing straight through shows that the atom is mostly empty space — the alpha particles rarely come close enough to anything to be deflected.

The few large-angle deflections show that the positive charge and nearly all the mass of the atom are concentrated in a very small, dense nucleus. Only an alpha particle heading almost directly at such a nucleus experiences a large enough repulsive force to be turned back; since the nucleus is tiny, this happens very rarely.

## Exercises

**Q1.** State the number of protons and neutrons in the nuclide ${}^{60}_{\ 27}\mathrm{Co}$.

**A1.** Protons $=Z=27$. Neutrons $=A-Z=60-27=33$.

**Q2.** Explain what is meant by an isotope, and state why isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties.

**A2.** Isotopes are atoms of the same element (the same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, hence different nucleon numbers. Chemical properties depend on the number and arrangement of the electrons, which is the same for all isotopes of an element, so their chemistry is identical.

**Q3.** A radioactive isotope has a half-life of $6.0$ hours and an initial corrected count rate of $640$ counts/minute. What is the corrected count rate after $18$ hours?

**A3.** $18$ hours is $\dfrac{18}{6.0}=3$ half-lives, so the count rate is $640\times\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^3=640\times\dfrac{1}{8}=80$ counts/minute.

**Q4.** Complete the decay equation: ${}^{214}_{\ 82}\mathrm{Pb}\rightarrow{}^{214}_{\ 83}\mathrm{Bi}+\ ?$

**A4.** The nucleon number is unchanged and the proton number increases by 1, so this is beta decay and the missing particle is ${}^{\ 0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$, a beta particle (electron).

**Q5.** Explain why the count rate from a radioactive source should be measured over a long time and corrected for background radiation.

**A5.** Radioactive decay is random, so the count rate fluctuates from moment to moment; averaging over a long time gives a reliable value. Background radiation is always present and is counted by the detector as well, so it must be measured separately and subtracted to give the count rate due to the source alone.

**Q6.** Explain why alpha radiation is the most strongly ionising but the least penetrating of the three types.

**A6.** An alpha particle is relatively large and carries a charge of $+2$, so it interacts strongly with the atoms it passes and readily removes electrons from them — hence strong ionisation. Because it loses energy rapidly in these interactions, it travels only a few centimetres in air and is stopped by a sheet of paper.

**Q7.** A source emits gamma radiation and has a half-life of $6$ hours. Explain why it is suitable for use as a medical tracer, and state one safety precaution.

**A7.** Gamma is weakly ionising but highly penetrating, so it can escape the body and be detected by a camera outside, giving an image without surgery. A half-life of a few hours is long enough to carry out the scan but short enough that the activity in the patient falls to a low level quickly. A precaution: the source should be handled with tongs, kept behind lead shielding, and the exposure time kept as short as possible.

**Q8.** Compare nuclear fission and nuclear fusion, stating one similarity and one difference.

**A8.** Similarity: both release energy because the products have a slightly smaller total mass than the reactants. Difference: fission splits a large, unstable nucleus into smaller nuclei, whereas fusion joins two small nuclei to make a larger one; fission is used in nuclear power stations, while fusion powers the stars.

**Q9.** An isotope has a half-life of $8.0$ days. A sample initially contains $4.8\times 10^{20}$ undecayed nuclei. How many remain after $24$ days?

**A9.** $24$ days is $3$ half-lives, so the number remaining is $4.8\times 10^{20}\times\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^3=4.8\times 10^{20}\times\dfrac{1}{8}=6.0\times 10^{19}$ nuclei.

**Q10.** In an electric field, alpha particles are deflected slightly towards the negative plate and beta particles strongly towards the positive plate. Explain both observations.

**A10.** Alpha particles are positively charged, so they are attracted to the negative plate; but they are about 7000 times more massive than an electron, so they are deflected only slightly. Beta particles are electrons, negatively charged, so they are attracted to the positive plate; their very small mass means the same force produces a much larger acceleration, so they are deflected strongly.
