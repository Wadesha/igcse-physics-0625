## Notes

### What a wave is

A wave transfers **energy** from one place to another without transferring **matter**. After a wave has passed, the particles of the medium are back where they started — only the energy has moved on.

Two families:

- **Transverse** waves: the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Examples: all electromagnetic waves, water waves, waves on a rope, seismic S-waves.
- **Longitudinal** waves: the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer, producing **compressions** (particles crowded) and **rarefactions** (particles spread out). Examples: sound waves in air, seismic P-waves.

### Describing a wave

| Term | Meaning | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| amplitude $A$ | maximum displacement from the rest position | m |
| wavelength $\lambda$ | distance between consecutive crests (or compressions) | m |
| frequency $f$ | number of complete waves passing a point each second | Hz |
| period $T$ | time for one complete wave to pass a point | s |
| wave speed $v$ | distance travelled by the wave energy per second | m/s |

They are linked by the **wave equation**:

$$v=f\lambda \qquad\text{and}\qquad T=\frac{1}{f}$$

A **wavefront** is a line (or surface) joining points that are at the same stage of oscillation; the direction of energy transfer is perpendicular to the wavefront.

### Reflection, refraction and diffraction

**Reflection** — the wave bounces off a boundary. For light at a plane mirror, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, with both angles measured from the **normal** (the line at $90^\circ$ to the surface). The image in a plane mirror is virtual, upright, the same size as the object, and laterally inverted (left–right reversed).

**Refraction** — the wave changes speed as it crosses a boundary, and changes direction unless it meets the boundary along the normal. Light slows down on entering a denser medium such as glass, bending **towards** the normal; on leaving it speeds up and bends **away** from the normal. The frequency stays constant, so the wavelength changes.

**Diffraction** — the wave spreads out as it passes through a gap or past an edge. It is most noticeable when the wavelength is comparable to the size of the gap (or obstacle): a long wavelength diffracts strongly through a narrow gap, while a short wavelength passes through with little spreading.

A **ripple tank** is used to show all three with water waves: a barrier reflects them, a sloping region of shallow water refracts them (they slow down), and a narrow gap diffracts them.

### Refractive index and total internal reflection

The **refractive index** $n$ of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum (or air) to its speed in the medium. Snell's law in the form used here is:

$$n=\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}$$

where $i$ is the angle in air and $r$ is the angle in the medium. A larger $n$ means more bending.

When light travels from a dense medium (glass) into a less dense one (air), it bends away from the normal. At a certain angle of incidence the refracted ray would leave along the boundary: that angle is the **critical angle** $c$, given by

$$\sin c=\frac{1}{n}$$

For angles of incidence **greater** than $c$, no light escapes at all — it is all reflected back inside. This is **total internal reflection** (TIR), and it requires the light to be travelling from the denser medium towards the less dense one.

Optical fibres use TIR: light is trapped inside a thin glass fibre by repeated total internal reflection, so it can carry signals around bends. Uses include endoscopes (keyhole surgery) and high-capacity telephone and internet cables.

### Lenses

A **converging** (convex) lens brings a parallel beam to a point; a **diverging** (concave) lens spreads it out.

Key terms: the **principal axis** is the line through the centre of the lens perpendicular to it; the **principal focus** $F$ is where rays parallel to the principal axis converge; the **focal length** $f$ is the distance from the lens centre to $F$.

For a converging lens, three standard rays locate the image:

1. A ray parallel to the principal axis refracts through the principal focus.
2. A ray through the centre of the lens continues undeviated.
3. A ray through the principal focus emerges parallel to the principal axis.

The image depends on the object distance:

- Object beyond $2F$: real, inverted, diminished (a camera).
- Object between $F$ and $2F$: real, inverted, magnified (a projector).
- Object closer than $F$: **virtual**, upright, magnified — the rays never actually meet, and the lens acts as a **magnifying glass**.

A **real** image can be caught on a screen; a **virtual** image cannot. Long sight (the eye focuses behind the retina) is corrected with a converging lens; short sight (focusing in front of the retina) with a diverging lens.

### Dispersion and colour

White light is a mixture of colours. A prism disperses it because the refractive index of glass is slightly different for each wavelength: violet is refracted most and red least.

The visible spectrum, in order of **increasing wavelength** (decreasing frequency): violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red. Light of a single frequency is described as **monochromatic**.

### The electromagnetic spectrum

All electromagnetic waves are transverse, travel at $3.0\times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in a vacuum, and can travel through a vacuum. They differ only in wavelength and frequency.

| Region | Typical uses | Main harmful effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| radio waves | radio and television broadcasting | — |
| microwaves | satellite communication, mobile phones, microwave ovens | internal heating of body cells |
| infrared | remote controls, thermal imaging, heaters | skin burns |
| visible light | vision, photography, optical fibres | — |
| ultraviolet | fluorescent tubes, sterilising water, tanning | skin cancer, eye damage |
| X-rays | medical imaging, security scanners | cell damage, mutations |
| gamma rays | sterilising equipment, treating cancer | cell damage, mutations |

Note that microwaves are used for satellite communication because they pass through the ionosphere, unlike longer radio waves.

**Analogue** signals vary continuously; **digital** signals take only discrete values (usually 0 and 1). Digital signals resist noise far better, can be regenerated exactly, and can carry more information in the same bandwidth.

### Sound

Sound is produced by a vibrating source and is a **longitudinal** wave: it consists of compressions and rarefactions travelling through a medium.

- A **medium is required** — sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
- The speed of sound in air is about $330$–$350\ \mathrm{m/s}$; it travels faster in liquids and faster still in solids, where the particles are closer together.
- Humans hear frequencies from about $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to $20\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
- **Loudness** depends on amplitude; **pitch** depends on frequency.

To measure the speed of sound: stand a known distance (say $300\ \mathrm{m}$) from a large wall, clap, and time 20 echoes; or use two microphones a known distance apart connected to a timer that starts and stops as the sound passes each one. Then $v=\dfrac{d}{t}$.

An **echo** is a reflected sound; it must arrive at least about $0.1\ \mathrm{s}$ after the original to be heard separately. **Ultrasound** is sound with a frequency above $20\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}$; it is used for scanning (prenatal scans), sonar (depth sounding and fish finding), and cleaning delicate instruments.

## Examples

### Example 1 (Using the wave equation)

A wave on a string has a frequency of $40\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and a wavelength of $0.25\ \mathrm{m}$. Calculate its speed.

**Solution**

$$v=f\lambda=40\times 0.25=10\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

### Example 2 (Refraction and critical angle)

Light travels from air into glass with an angle of incidence of $40^\circ$ and an angle of refraction of $25^\circ$. Calculate the refractive index of the glass, and hence the critical angle for glass–air.

**Solution**

$$n=\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}=\frac{\sin 40^\circ}{\sin 25^\circ}=\frac{0.643}{0.423}=1.52$$

For the glass–air boundary:

$$\sin c=\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{1.52}=0.658\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad c=\sin^{-1}(0.658)\approx 41^\circ$$

So rays meeting the glass–air boundary at more than about $41^\circ$ are totally internally reflected.

### Example 3 (Speed of sound by echo)

A student stands $170\ \mathrm{m}$ from a cliff and claps. She hears the echo $1.0\ \mathrm{s}$ later. Calculate the speed of sound.

**Solution**

The sound travels to the cliff and back, a total distance of $2\times 170=340\ \mathrm{m}$, in $1.0\ \mathrm{s}$.

$$v=\frac{d}{t}=\frac{340}{1.0}=340\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

## Exercises

**Q1.** A sound wave has a frequency of $500\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and travels at $340\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Calculate its wavelength.

**A1.** $\lambda=\dfrac{v}{f}=\dfrac{340}{500}=0.68\ \mathrm{m}$.

**Q2.** A water wave has a wavelength of $0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ and a speed of $2.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Calculate its frequency and its period.

**A2.** $f=\dfrac{v}{\lambda}=\dfrac{2.0}{0.40}=5.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$. $T=\dfrac{1}{f}=\dfrac{1}{5.0}=0.20\ \mathrm{s}$.

**Q3.** State the difference between a transverse and a longitudinal wave, and give one example of each.

**A3.** In a transverse wave the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer (for example light, or a water wave). In a longitudinal wave the oscillation is parallel to the direction of energy transfer (for example sound in air).

**Q4.** Light strikes a plane mirror at an angle of incidence of $35^\circ$. State the angle of reflection and describe the image formed.

**A4.** The angle of reflection is $35^\circ$ (measured from the normal). The image is virtual, upright, the same size as the object, and laterally inverted.

**Q5.** Explain why total internal reflection cannot occur when light travels from air into glass.

**A5.** TIR requires light to be travelling from a denser medium into a less dense one so that it bends away from the normal and can reach a critical angle. Travelling from air into glass, the light bends towards the normal, so the angle of refraction is always smaller than the angle of incidence and a critical angle is never reached.

**Q6.** An object is placed closer to a converging lens than its focal length. Describe the image formed and state one use of this arrangement.

**A6.** The image is virtual, upright and magnified. The rays diverge after the lens, so they never meet and the image cannot be caught on a screen. This is the arrangement used in a magnifying glass.

**Q7.** Explain why diffraction is more noticeable for sound waves than for light waves in everyday situations.

**A7.** Diffraction is strongest when the wavelength is comparable to the size of the gap or obstacle. Sound wavelengths in air are of the order of a metre, similar to doorways and corners, so sound spreads around them easily. Light has a wavelength of less than a millionth of a metre, so it diffracts only appreciably through extremely small apertures.

**Q8.** A ray of light enters a glass block at an angle of incidence of $50^\circ$. The refractive index of the glass is $1.50$. Calculate the angle of refraction.

**A8.** $\sin r=\dfrac{\sin i}{n}=\dfrac{\sin 50^\circ}{1.50}=\dfrac{0.766}{1.50}=0.511$, so $r=\sin^{-1}(0.511)\approx 31^\circ$.

**Q9.** Give two reasons why digital signals are preferred to analogue signals for long-distance communication.

**A9.** Digital signals are far less affected by noise, because a receiver only has to distinguish between two levels rather than reproduce an exact waveform; and they can be regenerated (amplified and reshaped) exactly at each repeater, so the signal does not degrade with distance.

**Q10.** Explain why an astronaut on the Moon cannot hear a colleague hammering, even though they can see it.

**A10.** Sound needs a medium to travel through, and the Moon's surface has no atmosphere, so the vibrations cannot reach the astronaut's ear. Light is an electromagnetic wave and needs no medium, so it travels through the vacuum and the hammering can be seen.
