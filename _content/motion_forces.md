# Motion, Forces & Energy

> Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 — Topic 1. Measurement, scalars and vectors, motion graphs, forces and Newton's laws, moments, momentum, energy stores and transfers, work and power, and pressure. It is the largest topic in the syllabus and underpins every other one.

## Specification

- Understand that a **scalar** has magnitude only while a **vector** has magnitude and direction; know that distance, speed, mass, energy and time are scalars, and that displacement, velocity, force, weight, acceleration and momentum are vectors.
- Define speed as distance travelled per unit time and velocity as speed in a given direction; recall and use $v=\dfrac{d}{t}$.

- State that the acceleration of free fall $g$ near the Earth is approximately constant at about $9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$; describe the motion of falling objects with and without air resistance, including terminal velocity.
- State that mass is a measure of the quantity of matter and that weight is a gravitational force; define gravitational field strength as force per unit mass; recall and use $W=mg$.
- Define density as mass per unit volume; recall and use $\rho=\dfrac{m}{V}$; describe how to determine the density of a liquid and of a regular or irregular solid; determine whether an object floats.
- Know that forces may change the size, shape or motion of an object; determine the resultant of two or more forces; state that a resultant force changes the velocity of an object.
- Sketch, plot and interpret load–extension graphs; define the spring constant as force per unit extension; recall and use $F=kx$; define the **limit of proportionality**.
- Recall and use $F=ma$; describe, qualitatively, motion in a circular path due to a force perpendicular to the velocity.
- Describe solid friction; know that drag acts on an object moving through a fluid and increases with speed.
- Define the moment of a force; recall and use moment $=F\times d$; state the principle of moments for an object in equilibrium.
- State what is meant by centre of gravity; describe an experiment to find it and the effect of its position on stability.
- Define momentum as mass × velocity and impulse as force × time; recall and use $p=mv$ and define resultant force as change in momentum per unit time.
- State that energy may be stored as kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic (strain), nuclear, electrostatic and internal (thermal); describe transfers between stores and use the principle of conservation of energy.
- Recall and use $E_k=\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and $\Delta E_p=mg\Delta h$.
- Understand that work done is the energy transferred; recall and use $W=Fs$; define power as work done per unit time and recall and use $P=\dfrac{W}{t}$.
- Define efficiency as the ratio of useful output energy (or power) to total input energy (or power).
- Define pressure as force per unit area; recall and use $p=\dfrac{F}{A}$; recall and use the change in pressure in a liquid $\Delta p=\rho g\Delta h$.

## Notes

### Measuring length, volume and time

Common instruments and their typical resolution:

- **Metre rule / ruler** — length, to the nearest millimetre. Read it at eye level to avoid parallax error.
- **Measuring cylinder** — volume of a liquid; read the bottom of the meniscus.
- **Clock / stopwatch** — time; for a short interval, time several repetitions and divide (this averages out reaction-time error).

To measure a **small distance** accurately (for example the thickness of one sheet of paper), measure many identical items together and divide by the count. To measure a **short time** (for example one swing of a pendulum), time 20 swings and divide by 20.

### Scalars and vectors

A **scalar** quantity is fully described by a magnitude (a number and a unit). A **vector** quantity needs both a magnitude and a direction.

| Scalars | Vectors |
| --- | --- |
| distance, speed, mass, time, energy, temperature | displacement, velocity, force, weight, acceleration, momentum |

Adding vectors that lie along the same straight line is straightforward: forces in the same direction add; forces in opposite directions subtract. Two vectors at an angle are combined using a scale drawing (a vector triangle) or by calculation.

### Speed, velocity and motion graphs

Average **speed** is total distance divided by total time:

$$v=\frac{d}{t}$$

**Velocity** is speed in a stated direction; an object moving at constant speed round a circle has a continuously changing velocity because its direction changes.

**Distance–time graph:** the gradient equals the speed. A horizontal line means the object is stationary; a straight sloping line means constant speed; a curve means the speed is changing.

**Speed–time graph:** the gradient equals the acceleration; the **area under the graph** equals the distance travelled. A horizontal line means constant speed (zero acceleration).

### Acceleration and free fall

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity:

$$a=\frac{\Delta v}{t}$$

Its unit is $\mathrm{m/s^2}$. A deceleration is simply a negative acceleration.

Near the Earth's surface, an object in free fall accelerates at approximately $g=9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (often rounded to $10\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ in examination questions). This means that, ignoring air resistance, its velocity increases by about $9.8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ every second.

With air resistance, the drag force grows as the speed increases. Eventually drag equals the weight, the resultant force becomes zero, and the object stops accelerating — it has reached its **terminal velocity**.

### Mass, weight and gravitational field strength

**Mass** is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms; it does not change when the object moves. **Weight** is the gravitational force acting on the object, measured in newtons; it does change from planet to planet.

Gravitational field strength $g$ is the force per unit mass, and

$$W=mg$$

So a $60\ \mathrm{kg}$ person has a weight of about $60\times 9.8=588\ \mathrm{N}$ on the Earth, but only about $96\ \mathrm{N}$ on the Moon where $g\approx 1.6\ \mathrm{N/kg}$.

### Density

Density is mass per unit volume:

$$\rho=\frac{m}{V}$$

Typical units are $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ or $\mathrm{g/cm^3}$; note that $1\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

To find the density of a **regular solid**, measure its dimensions, calculate its volume, and weigh it. For an **irregular solid**, submerge it in a measuring cylinder (or a displacement can) and use the rise in level as its volume. For a **liquid**, weigh an empty measuring cylinder, add a known volume of liquid, and subtract.

An object floats in a fluid if its density is less than the fluid's density; a liquid of lower density floats on top of a liquid of higher density.

### Forces, Hooke's law and the limit of proportionality

A force can change the size, the shape, or the motion of an object. Forces are measured with a newton meter (spring balance).

Stretching a spring: the **extension** is the increase in length, $x = \text{loaded length} - \text{original length}$. Provided the **limit of proportionality** is not exceeded, extension is proportional to the load:

$$F=kx$$

where $k$ is the spring constant in $\mathrm{N/m}$. On a load–extension graph the relationship is a straight line through the origin up to the limit of proportionality; beyond it, the graph curves and the spring is permanently deformed.

### Resultant force, $F=ma$ and circular motion

The **resultant force** is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting together.

- If the resultant force is zero, an object either stays at rest or continues at constant velocity.
- If the resultant force is not zero, the object accelerates in the direction of that resultant force.

$$F=ma$$

For motion in a circle at constant speed, the velocity is continuously changing direction, so the object is accelerating. The required force is directed towards the centre of the circle (a centripetal force) and is perpendicular to the velocity.

### Friction and terminal velocity

**Solid friction** is the force between two surfaces that opposes sliding. **Drag** is the frictional force on an object moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas); it increases with speed and depends on the object's shape and the fluid's density.

This is why a falling object reaches terminal velocity: drag increases with speed until it balances the weight, at which point the resultant force is zero.

### Moments and equilibrium

The **moment** of a force measures its turning effect about a pivot:

$$\text{moment}=F\times d$$

where $d$ is the **perpendicular** distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. The unit is $\mathrm{N\,m}$.

**Principle of moments:** for an object in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that point.

### Centre of gravity and stability

The **centre of gravity** is the point through which the entire weight of an object appears to act. For a uniform shape it is at the geometric centre.

To find the centre of gravity of a flat irregular lamina: suspend it freely from a point, hang a plumb line from the same point, and mark the vertical line; repeat from a second point. The centre of gravity is where the two lines cross.

An object topples when the line of action of its weight falls outside its base. So stability is improved by **lowering the centre of gravity** and **widening the base**.

### Momentum and impulse

**Momentum** is mass × velocity:

$$p=mv$$

Momentum is a vector, and in the absence of external forces total momentum is conserved in collisions and explosions.

**Impulse** is force × time and equals the change in momentum. Rearranging gives a useful form of Newton's second law:

$$F=\frac{\Delta p}{t}$$

so the resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum. A larger stopping time (crumple zones, airbags, bending your knees when landing) reduces the force for the same change in momentum.

### Energy stores and transfers

Energy may be stored as kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic (strain), nuclear, electrostatic and internal (thermal). Energy is transferred between stores by forces (mechanical work), by electric currents (electrical work), by heating, and by waves such as light and sound.

The **principle of conservation of energy** states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between stores. The total energy of a closed system is constant; "lost" energy is usually dissipated as internal (thermal) energy of the surroundings.

$$E_k=\frac{1}{2}mv^2\qquad\qquad \Delta E_p=mg\Delta h$$

### Work, power and efficiency

**Work done** equals the energy transferred. For a force moving an object:

$$W=Fs$$

where $s$ is the distance moved **in the direction of the force**. The unit is the joule (J); $1\ \mathrm{J}$ is the work done when a force of $1\ \mathrm{N}$ moves an object $1\ \mathrm{m}$.

**Power** is the rate of doing work (or of transferring energy):

$$P=\frac{W}{t}=\frac{E}{t}$$

The unit is the watt (W), where $1\ \mathrm{W}=1\ \mathrm{J/s}$.

**Efficiency** is the fraction of the input that ends up as useful output:

$$\text{efficiency}=\frac{\text{useful energy output}}{\text{total energy input}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{\text{useful power output}}{\text{total power input}}$$

### Pressure

Pressure is force per unit area:

$$p=\frac{F}{A}$$

The unit is the pascal (Pa), where $1\ \mathrm{Pa}=1\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$. A large area gives a small pressure for the same force (snowshoes, wide tyres); a small area gives a large pressure (a sharp knife, a drawing pin).

In a liquid, pressure increases with depth:

$$\Delta p=\rho g\Delta h$$

Pressure in a liquid acts equally in all directions and depends on depth and density, not on the shape or width of the container.

## Examples

### Example 1 (Reading a speed–time graph)

A car accelerates from rest to $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in $8\ \mathrm{s}$, travels at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for $12\ \mathrm{s}$, then brakes to rest in $5\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate (a) the initial acceleration, (b) the total distance travelled.

**Solution**

(a) The acceleration is the gradient of the first section:

$$a=\frac{\Delta v}{t}=\frac{20-0}{8}=2.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$

(b) The total distance is the area under the whole graph — a triangle, a rectangle, then a triangle:

$$\text{triangle}=\tfrac{1}{2}\times 8\times 20=80\ \mathrm{m}$$
$$\text{rectangle}=12\times 20=240\ \mathrm{m}$$
$$\text{triangle}=\tfrac{1}{2}\times 5\times 20=50\ \mathrm{m}$$

Total distance $=80+240+50=370\ \mathrm{m}$.

### Example 2 (Density by displacement)

A stone has a mass of $189\ \mathrm{g}$. It is lowered into a measuring cylinder containing $150\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of water, and the level rises to $220\ \mathrm{cm^3}$. Calculate the density of the stone in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, and state whether it will float in water.

**Solution**

Volume of the stone $=220-150=70\ \mathrm{cm^3}$.

$$\rho=\frac{m}{V}=\frac{189}{70}=2.7\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$$

Convert: $2.7\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}=2700\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

Water has a density of $1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Since $2700>1000$, the stone sinks.

### Example 3 (Conservation of momentum)

A trolley of mass $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ moving at $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ collides head-on with a stationary trolley of mass $1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$. The trolleys stick together. Calculate their common velocity after the collision.

**Solution**

Total momentum before $=m_1u_1+m_2u_2=(2.0\times 3.0)+(1.0\times 0)=6.0\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$.

After the collision the combined mass is $3.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, so by conservation of momentum:

$$6.0=3.0\times v\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad v=2.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

in the original direction of motion.

## Exercises

**Q1.** A cyclist travels $450\ \mathrm{m}$ in $30\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate the average speed.

**A1.** $v=\dfrac{d}{t}=\dfrac{450}{30}=15\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

**Q2.** A car increases its velocity from $12\ \mathrm{m/s}$ to $30\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in $6\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate its acceleration.

**A2.** $a=\dfrac{\Delta v}{t}=\dfrac{30-12}{6}=\dfrac{18}{6}=3\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

**Q3.** An object of mass $5.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ is taken to the Moon where $g=1.6\ \mathrm{N/kg}$. State its mass and calculate its weight there.

**A3.** Mass is unchanged: $5.0\ \mathrm{kg}$. Weight $=mg=5.0\times 1.6=8.0\ \mathrm{N}$.

**Q4.** A metal block measures $4.0\ \mathrm{cm}\times 5.0\ \mathrm{cm}\times 2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ and has a mass of $320\ \mathrm{g}$. Calculate its density in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

**A4.** Volume $=4.0\times 5.0\times 2.0=40\ \mathrm{cm^3}$. Density $=\dfrac{320}{40}=8.0\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}=8000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

**Q5.** A spring of original length $12.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ extends to $17.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ when a $10\ \mathrm{N}$ load is hung from it. Calculate the spring constant.

**A5.** Extension $x=17.0-12.0=5.0\ \mathrm{cm}=0.050\ \mathrm{m}$. $k=\dfrac{F}{x}=\dfrac{10}{0.050}=200\ \mathrm{N/m}$.

**Q6.** A resultant force of $24\ \mathrm{N}$ acts on a mass of $6.0\ \mathrm{kg}$. Calculate the acceleration produced.

**A6.** $a=\dfrac{F}{m}=\dfrac{24}{6.0}=4.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

**Q7.** A uniform metre rule is pivoted at its centre (the $50\ \mathrm{cm}$ mark). A $4.0\ \mathrm{N}$ weight is hung at the $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ mark. What weight must be hung at the $80\ \mathrm{cm}$ mark to balance the rule?

**A7.** Taking moments about the pivot: anticlockwise moment $=4.0\times (50-10)=4.0\times 40=160\ \mathrm{N\,cm}$. Clockwise: $W\times (80-50)=W\times 30$. For equilibrium $30W=160$, so $W=5.3\ \mathrm{N}$.

**Q8.** A crane lifts a $500\ \mathrm{kg}$ load through a height of $12\ \mathrm{m}$ in $20\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate the useful power output. Use $g=9.8\ \mathrm{N/kg}$.

**A8.** Energy transferred $=mg\Delta h=500\times 9.8\times 12=58\,800\ \mathrm{J}$. $P=\dfrac{E}{t}=\dfrac{58\,800}{20}=2940\ \mathrm{W}\approx 2.9\ \mathrm{kW}$.

**Q9.** An electric motor takes in $800\ \mathrm{J}$ of electrical energy and delivers $600\ \mathrm{J}$ of useful kinetic energy. Calculate its efficiency.

**A9.** Efficiency $=\dfrac{600}{800}=0.75$, i.e. $75\%$. The remaining $200\ \mathrm{J}$ is dissipated as thermal energy.

**Q10.** A rectangular block of weight $120\ \mathrm{N}$ rests on a face of area $0.40\ \mathrm{m^2}$. It is then stood on a smaller face of area $0.10\ \mathrm{m^2}$. Calculate the pressure in each case.

**A10.** $p=\dfrac{F}{A}$: $\dfrac{120}{0.40}=300\ \mathrm{Pa}$, and $\dfrac{120}{0.10}=1200\ \mathrm{Pa}$. The smaller contact area gives four times the pressure.
