Motion, Forces & Energy

Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 — Topic 1. Measurement, scalars and vectors, motion graphs, forces and Newton's laws, moments, momentum, energy stores and transfers, work and power, and pressure. It is the largest topic in the syllabus and underpins every other one.

Specification · What students need to learn

  • Describe the use of rulers, measuring cylinders and clocks to measure length, volume and time; determine an average value for a small distance and for a short interval of time.

  • Understand that a scalar has magnitude only while a vector has magnitude and direction; know that distance, speed, mass, energy and time are scalars, and that displacement, velocity, force, weight, acceleration and momentum are vectors.

  • Define speed as distance travelled per unit time and velocity as speed in a given direction; recall and use $v=\dfrac{d}{t}$.

  • Sketch, plot and interpret distance–time and speed–time graphs; calculate speed from the gradient of a distance–time graph, acceleration from the gradient of a speed–time graph, and distance from the area under a speed–time graph.

  • Define acceleration as change in velocity per unit time; recall and use $a=\dfrac{\Delta v}{t}$; know that a deceleration is a negative acceleration.

  • State that the acceleration of free fall $g$ near the Earth is approximately constant at about $9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$; describe the motion of falling objects with and without air resistance, including terminal velocity.

  • State that mass is a measure of the quantity of matter and that weight is a gravitational force; define gravitational field strength as force per unit mass; recall and use $W=mg$.

  • Define density as mass per unit volume; recall and use $\rho=\dfrac{m}{V}$; describe how to determine the density of a liquid and of a regular or irregular solid; determine whether an object floats.

  • Know that forces may change the size, shape or motion of an object; determine the resultant of two or more forces; state that a resultant force changes the velocity of an object.

  • Sketch, plot and interpret load–extension graphs; define the spring constant as force per unit extension; recall and use $F=kx$; define the limit of proportionality.

  • Recall and use $F=ma$; describe, qualitatively, motion in a circular path due to a force perpendicular to the velocity.

  • Describe solid friction; know that drag acts on an object moving through a fluid and increases with speed.

  • Define the moment of a force; recall and use moment $=F\times d$; state the principle of moments for an object in equilibrium.

  • State what is meant by centre of gravity; describe an experiment to find it and the effect of its position on stability.

  • Define momentum as mass × velocity and impulse as force × time; recall and use $p=mv$ and define resultant force as change in momentum per unit time.

  • State that energy may be stored as kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic (strain), nuclear, electrostatic and internal (thermal); describe transfers between stores and use the principle of conservation of energy.

  • Recall and use $E_k=\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ and $\Delta E_p=mg\Delta h$.

  • Understand that work done is the energy transferred; recall and use $W=Fs$; define power as work done per unit time and recall and use $P=\dfrac{W}{t}$.

  • Define efficiency as the ratio of useful output energy (or power) to total input energy (or power).

  • Define pressure as force per unit area; recall and use $p=\dfrac{F}{A}$; recall and use the change in pressure in a liquid $\Delta p=\rho g\Delta h$.

Notes

Measuring length, volume and time

Common instruments and their typical resolution:

  • Metre rule / ruler — length, to the nearest millimetre. Read it at eye level to avoid parallax error.
  • Measuring cylinder — volume of a liquid; read the bottom of the meniscus.
  • Clock / stopwatch — time; for a short interval, time several repetitions and divide (this averages out reaction-time error).

To measure a small distance accurately (for example the thickness of one sheet of paper), measure many identical items together and divide by the count. To measure a short time (for example one swing of a pendulum), time 20 swings and divide by 20.

Scalars and vectors

A scalar quantity is fully described by a magnitude (a number and a unit). A vector quantity needs both a magnitude and a direction.

ScalarsVectors
distance, speed, mass, time, energy, temperaturedisplacement, velocity, force, weight, acceleration, momentum

Adding vectors that lie along the same straight line is straightforward: forces in the same direction add; forces in opposite directions subtract. Two vectors at an angle are combined using a scale drawing (a vector triangle) or by calculation.

Speed, velocity and motion graphs

Average speed is total distance divided by total time:

$$v=\frac{d}{t}$$

Velocity is speed in a stated direction; an object moving at constant speed round a circle has a continuously changing velocity because its direction changes.

Distance–time graph: the gradient equals the speed. A horizontal line means the object is stationary; a straight sloping line means constant speed; a curve means the speed is changing.

Speed–time graph: the gradient equals the acceleration; the area under the graph equals the distance travelled. A horizontal line means constant speed (zero acceleration).

Acceleration and free fall

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity:

$$a=\frac{\Delta v}{t}$$

Its unit is $\mathrm{m/s^2}$. A deceleration is simply a negative acceleration.

Near the Earth's surface, an object in free fall accelerates at approximately $g=9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (often rounded to $10\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ in examination questions). This means that, ignoring air resistance, its velocity increases by about $9.8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ every second.

With air resistance, the drag force grows as the speed increases. Eventually drag equals the weight, the resultant force becomes zero, and the object stops accelerating — it has reached its terminal velocity.

Mass, weight and gravitational field strength

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms; it does not change when the object moves. Weight is the gravitational force acting on the object, measured in newtons; it does change from planet to planet.

Gravitational field strength $g$ is the force per unit mass, and

$$W=mg$$

So a $60\ \mathrm{kg}$ person has a weight of about $60\times 9.8=588\ \mathrm{N}$ on the Earth, but only about $96\ \mathrm{N}$ on the Moon where $g\approx 1.6\ \mathrm{N/kg}$.

Density

Density is mass per unit volume:

$$\rho=\frac{m}{V}$$

Typical units are $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ or $\mathrm{g/cm^3}$; note that $1\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

To find the density of a regular solid, measure its dimensions, calculate its volume, and weigh it. For an irregular solid, submerge it in a measuring cylinder (or a displacement can) and use the rise in level as its volume. For a liquid, weigh an empty measuring cylinder, add a known volume of liquid, and subtract.

An object floats in a fluid if its density is less than the fluid's density; a liquid of lower density floats on top of a liquid of higher density.

Forces, Hooke's law and the limit of proportionality

A force can change the size, the shape, or the motion of an object. Forces are measured with a newton meter (spring balance).

Stretching a spring: the extension is the increase in length, $x = \text{loaded length} - \text{original length}$. Provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded, extension is proportional to the load:

$$F=kx$$

where $k$ is the spring constant in $\mathrm{N/m}$. On a load–extension graph the relationship is a straight line through the origin up to the limit of proportionality; beyond it, the graph curves and the spring is permanently deformed.

Resultant force, $F=ma$ and circular motion

The resultant force is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting together.

  • If the resultant force is zero, an object either stays at rest or continues at constant velocity.
  • If the resultant force is not zero, the object accelerates in the direction of that resultant force.

$$F=ma$$

For motion in a circle at constant speed, the velocity is continuously changing direction, so the object is accelerating. The required force is directed towards the centre of the circle (a centripetal force) and is perpendicular to the velocity.

Friction and terminal velocity

Solid friction is the force between two surfaces that opposes sliding. Drag is the frictional force on an object moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas); it increases with speed and depends on the object's shape and the fluid's density.

This is why a falling object reaches terminal velocity: drag increases with speed until it balances the weight, at which point the resultant force is zero.

Moments and equilibrium

The moment of a force measures its turning effect about a pivot:

$$\text{moment}=F\times d$$

where $d$ is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. The unit is $\mathrm{N\,m}$.

Principle of moments: for an object in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that point.

Centre of gravity and stability

The centre of gravity is the point through which the entire weight of an object appears to act. For a uniform shape it is at the geometric centre.

To find the centre of gravity of a flat irregular lamina: suspend it freely from a point, hang a plumb line from the same point, and mark the vertical line; repeat from a second point. The centre of gravity is where the two lines cross.

An object topples when the line of action of its weight falls outside its base. So stability is improved by lowering the centre of gravity and widening the base.

Momentum and impulse

Momentum is mass × velocity:

$$p=mv$$

Momentum is a vector, and in the absence of external forces total momentum is conserved in collisions and explosions.

Impulse is force × time and equals the change in momentum. Rearranging gives a useful form of Newton's second law:

$$F=\frac{\Delta p}{t}$$

so the resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum. A larger stopping time (crumple zones, airbags, bending your knees when landing) reduces the force for the same change in momentum.

Energy stores and transfers

Energy may be stored as kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic (strain), nuclear, electrostatic and internal (thermal). Energy is transferred between stores by forces (mechanical work), by electric currents (electrical work), by heating, and by waves such as light and sound.

The principle of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between stores. The total energy of a closed system is constant; "lost" energy is usually dissipated as internal (thermal) energy of the surroundings.

$$E_k=\frac{1}{2}mv^2\qquad\qquad \Delta E_p=mg\Delta h$$

Work, power and efficiency

Work done equals the energy transferred. For a force moving an object:

$$W=Fs$$

where $s$ is the distance moved in the direction of the force. The unit is the joule (J); $1\ \mathrm{J}$ is the work done when a force of $1\ \mathrm{N}$ moves an object $1\ \mathrm{m}$.

Power is the rate of doing work (or of transferring energy):

$$P=\frac{W}{t}=\frac{E}{t}$$

The unit is the watt (W), where $1\ \mathrm{W}=1\ \mathrm{J/s}$.

Efficiency is the fraction of the input that ends up as useful output:

$$\text{efficiency}=\frac{\text{useful energy output}}{\text{total energy input}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{\text{useful power output}}{\text{total power input}}$$

Pressure

Pressure is force per unit area:

$$p=\frac{F}{A}$$

The unit is the pascal (Pa), where $1\ \mathrm{Pa}=1\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$. A large area gives a small pressure for the same force (snowshoes, wide tyres); a small area gives a large pressure (a sharp knife, a drawing pin).

In a liquid, pressure increases with depth:

$$\Delta p=\rho g\Delta h$$

Pressure in a liquid acts equally in all directions and depends on depth and density, not on the shape or width of the container.

Examples (3)

Example 1

A car accelerates from rest to $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in $8\ \mathrm{s}$, travels at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for $12\ \mathrm{s}$, then brakes to rest in $5\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate (a) the initial acceleration, (b) the total distance travelled.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

(a) The acceleration is the gradient of the first section:

$$a=\frac{\Delta v}{t}=\frac{20-0}{8}=2.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$

(b) The total distance is the area under the whole graph — a triangle, a rectangle, then a triangle:

$$\text{triangle}=\tfrac{1}{2}\times 8\times 20=80\ \mathrm{m}$$ $$\text{rectangle}=12\times 20=240\ \mathrm{m}$$ $$\text{triangle}=\tfrac{1}{2}\times 5\times 20=50\ \mathrm{m}$$

Total distance $=80+240+50=370\ \mathrm{m}$.

Example 2

A stone has a mass of $189\ \mathrm{g}$. It is lowered into a measuring cylinder containing $150\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of water, and the level rises to $220\ \mathrm{cm^3}$. Calculate the density of the stone in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, and state whether it will float in water.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

Volume of the stone $=220-150=70\ \mathrm{cm^3}$.

$$\rho=\frac{m}{V}=\frac{189}{70}=2.7\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$$

Convert: $2.7\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}=2700\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

Water has a density of $1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Since $2700>1000$, the stone sinks.

Example 3

A trolley of mass $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ moving at $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ collides head-on with a stationary trolley of mass $1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$. The trolleys stick together. Calculate their common velocity after the collision.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

Total momentum before $=m_1u_1+m_2u_2=(2.0\times 3.0)+(1.0\times 0)=6.0\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$.

After the collision the combined mass is $3.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, so by conservation of momentum:

$$6.0=3.0\times v\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad v=2.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

in the original direction of motion.

Thermal Physics

Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 — Topic 2. The kinetic particle model of matter, thermal expansion, temperature and specific heat capacity, changes of state, and the three methods of thermal energy transfer: conduction, convection and radiation.

Specification · What students need to learn

  • Know the distinguishing properties of solids, liquids and gases in terms of the arrangement and motion of particles, and of their density and compressibility.

  • Know the terms for changes of state: melting, freezing (solidification), boiling, condensation, evaporation and sublimation.

  • Describe the particle structure of solids, liquids and gases in terms of particle separation, arrangement and motion.

  • Describe the relationship between the motion of particles and temperature; know that the random motion of microscopic particles is evidenced by Brownian motion.

  • Describe, in terms of particles, the pressure of a gas and how it changes when the volume or the temperature changes.

  • Describe, qualitatively, the thermal expansion of solids, liquids and gases; explain applications and consequences such as the bimetallic strip.

  • Explain, in terms of the motion and arrangement of particles, the relative order of magnitude of expansion of solids, liquids and gases.

  • Know that a rise in the temperature of an object increases its internal energy; describe a temperature rise as an increase in the average kinetic energy of the particles.

  • Define specific heat capacity; recall and use $E=mc\Delta T$; describe experiments to measure it.

  • Describe melting and boiling in terms of energy input with no change in temperature; know the melting and boiling temperatures of water; describe condensation and solidification; describe evaporation in terms of the escape of the more energetic molecules and know that it causes cooling.

  • Describe the differences between boiling and evaporation, and how temperature, surface area and air movement over a surface affect the rate of evaporation.

  • Describe experiments to demonstrate conduction, convection and radiation; explain convection in liquids and gases in terms of density changes.

  • Know that thermal radiation is infrared radiation and that, unlike conduction and convection, it does not require a medium.

  • Describe the effect of surface colour and texture (black/dull versus white/shiny) on the emission, absorption and reflection of radiation.

  • Know that for an object to stay at a constant temperature the rate at which it receives energy must equal the rate at which it loses energy; know what happens when the two rates differ.

  • Explain everyday applications and consequences of conduction, convection and radiation.

The kinetic particle model explains all of this: matter is made of particles that are in constant random motion, and the temperature of a substance measures the average kinetic energy of its particles.

Brownian motion

Smoke particles viewed through a microscope jitter about in random, jerky paths. This is Brownian motion, and it is evidence that the particles of a fluid are themselves moving randomly: the visible smoke particles are bombarded unevenly on different sides by fast, invisible air (or water) molecules.

Two points examiners look for:

  • The motion is random — not caused by convection currents or by the smoke particles being "alive".
  • It is more vigorous at higher temperature, because the fluid molecules have more kinetic energy.

Gas pressure in terms of particles

The particles of a gas collide with the walls of their container; each collision exerts a tiny force, and the total force per unit area is the pressure.

  • Reducing the volume at constant temperature crowds the particles together, so collisions are more frequent and the pressure rises.
  • Raising the temperature makes the particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and with greater force, so the pressure rises.

Thermal expansion

When a substance is heated, its particles vibrate (or move) more vigorously and, on average, push each other slightly further apart. The substance expands.

For the same temperature rise, gases expand much more than liquids, and liquids expand slightly more than solids. This is because the particles in a gas are only weakly held together, so a small increase in motion produces a large increase in separation.

Consequences and uses:

  • Gaps must be left between lengths of railway track and in bridge expansion joints.
  • A bimetallic strip (two metals with different expansion rates bonded together) bends when heated and is used as a switch in thermostats and fire alarms.
  • Hot water in a glass jug can crack it if the inner surface expands before the outer one.

Temperature and internal energy

Internal energy is the total kinetic energy (from motion) plus potential energy (from separation) of all the particles in a substance.

When you heat an object and its temperature rises, you have increased the average kinetic energy of its particles. Note that internal energy is not the same as temperature: a large block of metal at $50^\circ\mathrm{C}$ holds far more internal energy than a small nail at $50^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because it contains far more particles.

Specific heat capacity

The specific heat capacity $c$ of a substance is the energy needed to raise the temperature of unit mass of it by one degree:

$$E=mc\Delta T$$

where $E$ is energy in joules, $m$ is mass in kg, $c$ is in $\mathrm{J/(kg\,^\circ C)}$ or $\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, and $\Delta T$ is the temperature rise.

Water has an unusually large specific heat capacity, about $4200\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,^\circ C)}$. This is why it is used as a coolant in car engines and in central heating, and why coastal areas have smaller temperature swings than inland areas.

To measure $c$ for a metal block: weigh the block, insert a heater and a thermometer, record the temperature rise $\Delta T$ over a timed interval, and measure the electrical energy supplied with a joulemeter (or with $E=VIt$ from an ammeter, voltmeter and stopwatch).

Pure water melts at $0^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and boils at $100^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at atmospheric pressure.

The crucial point: during melting and boiling the temperature does not rise even though energy is being supplied. The energy goes into increasing the separation of the particles (raising their potential energy) and breaking the bonds that hold them together, not into raising their average kinetic energy.

Evaporation versus boiling

Evaporation happens only at the surface, at any temperature, and is slow. Within a liquid the molecules have a range of speeds; the fastest ones near the surface can escape if they are moving upwards fast enough to overcome the attraction of the others. Because the most energetic molecules leave, the average kinetic energy of those left behind falls — so evaporation causes cooling.

The rate of evaporation increases when the temperature is higher, the surface area is larger, and air moves over the surface (carrying away the escaped molecules).

Boiling happens throughout the liquid, only at the boiling temperature, and is rapid — bubbles of vapour form inside the liquid.

Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a material without the material itself moving. In a metal, the free (delocalised) electrons gain kinetic energy where the metal is hot and diffuse to cooler regions, transferring energy quickly; lattice vibrations also pass energy along. This is why metals are good conductors and non-metals (wood, plastic, air) are poor ones.

Trapped air is a very poor conductor, which is why cavity wall insulation, loft insulation, fibreglass and wool are effective — they trap pockets of still air.

Convection

Convection occurs in fluids (liquids and gases). When part of a fluid is heated it expands, becomes less dense, and rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place. The result is a convection current that carries thermal energy around the whole fluid.

Convection needs a fluid, so it cannot occur in a solid, and it cannot occur in a vacuum.

Applications: hot water tanks draw off hot water from the top; refrigerators have their freezing compartment at the top so cold, dense air sinks; ventilation and sea breezes are convection currents.

Thermal radiation

Thermal radiation is infrared radiation. Unlike conduction and convection it needs no medium — it can travel through a vacuum, which is how the Sun's energy reaches the Earth.

Every object emits and absorbs thermal radiation. The rate depends on the surface:

  • A black, dull surface is a good emitter and a good absorber, and a poor reflector.
  • A white, shiny surface is a poor emitter and a poor absorber, and a good reflector.

Solar panels are black to absorb well; the inside of a vacuum flask is shiny to reduce emission; houses in hot climates are often painted white.

Constant temperature and the balance of rates

For an object's temperature to stay constant, the rate at which it receives energy must equal the rate at which it loses it. If the input rate exceeds the output rate the temperature rises; if it is less, the temperature falls. This is why a heater on a low setting does not necessarily make a room colder than a high setting — it simply settles at a lower equilibrium temperature.

The Earth's temperature works the same way: incoming radiation from the Sun is balanced by radiation emitted back into space. Changes to the atmosphere that reduce the outgoing rate cause the equilibrium temperature to rise.

Examples (3)

Example 1

An electric kettle contains $1.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Calculate the energy needed to bring the water to boiling point ($100^\circ\mathrm{C}$). Take $c=4200\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,^\circ C)}$.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

$\Delta T=100-20=80^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

$$E=mc\Delta T=1.5\times 4200\times 80=504\,000\ \mathrm{J}$$

So about $5.0\times 10^5\ \mathrm{J}$ (or $504\ \mathrm{kJ}$) is needed, ignoring the energy needed to heat the kettle itself and any losses.

Example 2

A sealed flask of air is heated from $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $70^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Explain, in terms of particles, why the pressure in the flask increases.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

Heating gives the air molecules more kinetic energy, so they move faster. Faster molecules collide with the walls of the flask more often and with a larger force each time. Pressure is the force per unit area on the walls, so more frequent and more forceful collisions produce a higher pressure. The volume is fixed and the mass of gas is fixed, so the increase comes entirely from the higher molecular speed.

Example 3

A satellite in space must both avoid overheating in sunlight and avoid losing too much energy when in the Earth's shadow. Explain why engineers use shiny, mirror-like surfaces and reflective foil blankets.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

A satellite is in a vacuum, so conduction and convection are impossible and radiation is the only way it can gain or lose energy. A shiny, mirror-like surface is a poor absorber, so it takes in little of the Sun's intense radiation; it is also a poor emitter, so it loses little of its own thermal energy when on the dark side. A black surface would absorb strongly in sunlight (overheating) and emit strongly in shadow (overcooling), so a shiny surface keeps the temperature far more stable.

Waves

Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 — Topic 3. The general properties of waves, reflection, refraction and diffraction; light (reflection, refraction, total internal reflection, lenses and dispersion) and the electromagnetic spectrum; and the production, nature and properties of sound.

Specification · What students need to learn

  • Know that waves transfer energy without transferring matter; describe wave motion in terms of the propagation of a disturbance that transfers energy.

  • Describe the features of a wave in terms of wavefront, wavelength, frequency, amplitude, period and wave speed; recall and use $v=f\lambda$.

  • Know that in a transverse wave the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer, and in a longitudinal wave it is parallel.

  • Describe how waves undergo reflection, refraction and diffraction; describe the use of a ripple tank; describe how wavelength and gap size affect diffraction, and how wavelength affects diffraction at an edge.

  • Light: define and use the terms normal, angle of incidence and angle of reflection; describe image formation by a plane mirror; state that for reflection the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection; use simple constructions, measurements and calculations.

  • Light: define and use the terms normal, angle of incidence and angle of refraction; describe the passage of light through a parallel-sided transparent block and through a prism; state the meaning of critical angle; describe internal reflection and total internal reflection.

  • Define refractive index $n$ as the ratio of the speeds of light in the two media; recall and use $n=\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r}$ and $\sin c=\dfrac{1}{n}$; describe the use of optical fibres, particularly in medicine and communications.

  • Lenses: describe the action of thin converging and thin diverging lenses on a parallel beam; define focal length, principal focus and principal axis; draw and use ray diagrams for the formation of a real image by a converging lens; describe the characteristics of an image (real/virtual, upright/inverted, magnified/diminished).

  • Know that a virtual image is formed by a converging lens when the object is closer than the focal length; draw and use the ray diagram for a magnifying glass; describe the use of a single lens as a magnifying glass, and in a camera and a projector; describe the correction of long sight and short sight.

  • Dispersion: describe the dispersion of light by a prism; know the seven colours of the visible spectrum in order of frequency and wavelength; recall that visible light of a single frequency is monochromatic.

  • Electromagnetic spectrum: know the main regions (radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays) in order of wavelength and frequency; know that all electromagnetic waves travel at the same high speed in a vacuum, $3.0\times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$; describe typical uses and harmful effects of each region; know that communication with artificial satellites uses microwaves; know the difference between digital and analogue signals and the benefits of digital signalling.

  • Sound: describe the production of sound by vibrating sources; describe its longitudinal nature; state the approximate range of audible frequencies ($20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to $20\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}$); know that a medium is needed to transmit sound; know that the speed of sound in air is about $330$–$350\ \mathrm{m/s}$; describe compression and rarefaction; know that sound travels faster in solids than in liquids, and faster in liquids than in gases.

  • Describe a method for measuring the speed of sound; describe how changes in amplitude and frequency affect loudness and pitch; describe an echo as the reflection of sound; define ultrasound and describe its uses.

Notes

What a wave is

A wave transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. After a wave has passed, the particles of the medium are back where they started — only the energy has moved on.

Two families:

  • Transverse waves: the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Examples: all electromagnetic waves, water waves, waves on a rope, seismic S-waves.
  • Longitudinal waves: the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer, producing compressions (particles crowded) and rarefactions (particles spread out). Examples: sound waves in air, seismic P-waves.

A wavefront is a line (or surface) joining points that are at the same stage of oscillation; the direction of energy transfer is perpendicular to the wavefront.

Reflection, refraction and diffraction

Reflection — the wave bounces off a boundary. For light at a plane mirror, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, with both angles measured from the normal (the line at $90^\circ$ to the surface). The image in a plane mirror is virtual, upright, the same size as the object, and laterally inverted (left–right reversed).

Refraction — the wave changes speed as it crosses a boundary, and changes direction unless it meets the boundary along the normal. Light slows down on entering a denser medium such as glass, bending towards the normal; on leaving it speeds up and bends away from the normal. The frequency stays constant, so the wavelength changes.

Diffraction — the wave spreads out as it passes through a gap or past an edge. It is most noticeable when the wavelength is comparable to the size of the gap (or obstacle): a long wavelength diffracts strongly through a narrow gap, while a short wavelength passes through with little spreading.

A ripple tank is used to show all three with water waves: a barrier reflects them, a sloping region of shallow water refracts them (they slow down), and a narrow gap diffracts them.

Refractive index and total internal reflection

The refractive index $n$ of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum (or air) to its speed in the medium. Snell's law in the form used here is:

$$n=\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}$$

where $i$ is the angle in air and $r$ is the angle in the medium. A larger $n$ means more bending.

When light travels from a dense medium (glass) into a less dense one (air), it bends away from the normal. At a certain angle of incidence the refracted ray would leave along the boundary: that angle is the critical angle $c$, given by

$$\sin c=\frac{1}{n}$$

For angles of incidence greater than $c$, no light escapes at all — it is all reflected back inside. This is total internal reflection (TIR), and it requires the light to be travelling from the denser medium towards the less dense one.

Optical fibres use TIR: light is trapped inside a thin glass fibre by repeated total internal reflection, so it can carry signals around bends. Uses include endoscopes (keyhole surgery) and high-capacity telephone and internet cables.

Lenses

A converging (convex) lens brings a parallel beam to a point; a diverging (concave) lens spreads it out.

Key terms: the principal axis is the line through the centre of the lens perpendicular to it; the principal focus $F$ is where rays parallel to the principal axis converge; the focal length $f$ is the distance from the lens centre to $F$.

For a converging lens, three standard rays locate the image:

  1. A ray parallel to the principal axis refracts through the principal focus.
  2. A ray through the centre of the lens continues undeviated.
  3. A ray through the principal focus emerges parallel to the principal axis.

The image depends on the object distance:

  • Object beyond $2F$: real, inverted, diminished (a camera).
  • Object between $F$ and $2F$: real, inverted, magnified (a projector).
  • Object closer than $F$: virtual, upright, magnified — the rays never actually meet, and the lens acts as a magnifying glass.

A real image can be caught on a screen; a virtual image cannot. Long sight (the eye focuses behind the retina) is corrected with a converging lens; short sight (focusing in front of the retina) with a diverging lens.

Dispersion and colour

White light is a mixture of colours. A prism disperses it because the refractive index of glass is slightly different for each wavelength: violet is refracted most and red least.

The visible spectrum, in order of increasing wavelength (decreasing frequency): violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red. Light of a single frequency is described as monochromatic.

The electromagnetic spectrum

All electromagnetic waves are transverse, travel at $3.0\times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in a vacuum, and can travel through a vacuum. They differ only in wavelength and frequency.

Sound

Sound is produced by a vibrating source and is a longitudinal wave: it consists of compressions and rarefactions travelling through a medium.

  • A medium is required — sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • The speed of sound in air is about $330$–$350\ \mathrm{m/s}$; it travels faster in liquids and faster still in solids, where the particles are closer together.
  • Humans hear frequencies from about $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to $20\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
  • Loudness depends on amplitude; pitch depends on frequency.

To measure the speed of sound: stand a known distance (say $300\ \mathrm{m}$) from a large wall, clap, and time 20 echoes; or use two microphones a known distance apart connected to a timer that starts and stops as the sound passes each one. Then $v=\dfrac{d}{t}$.

An echo is a reflected sound; it must arrive at least about $0.1\ \mathrm{s}$ after the original to be heard separately. Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above $20\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}$; it is used for scanning (prenatal scans), sonar (depth sounding and fish finding), and cleaning delicate instruments.

Notes

Magnetism

Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Induced magnetism is the temporary magnetism produced in a magnetic material when it is placed near a magnet — this is why an unmagnetised nail is attracted to either pole of a magnet.

  • Hard magnetic materials (steel) keep their magnetism and make permanent magnets.
  • Soft magnetic materials (iron) magnetise and demagnetise easily and are used in transformers and electromagnet cores.

A magnetic field is a region where a magnetic pole experiences a force. Its direction at any point is the direction of the force on a north pole placed there. Field lines run from north to south; where they are close together the field is strong, and field lines never cross. You can plot them with a small plotting compass.

Electrostatics and electric fields

There are two kinds of charge, positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charge is measured in coulombs (C).

Charging two solids by friction (for example a plastic rod rubbed with a cloth) works by transferring electrons: the material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged, and the one that loses them becomes equally positively charged. Charge is conserved overall.

  • Conductors (metals, graphite) contain free electrons that can move through the material.
  • Insulators (plastics, glass) have electrons that are bound to their atoms and cannot move freely.

An electric field is a region where a charge experiences a force. By convention its direction is the direction of the force on a positive charge, so field lines point away from positive charges and towards negative ones. A practical use of electrostatic charging is a paint sprayer, where the charged droplets repel each other to give a fine, even spray and are attracted to the earthed object.

Electric current

Current is the rate of flow of charge:

$$I=\frac{Q}{t}\qquad\text{or}\qquad Q=It$$

Current is measured with an ammeter connected in series; the unit is the ampere (A), where $1\ \mathrm{A}=1\ \mathrm{C/s}$.

Conventional current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal, even though the electrons in a metal actually drift in the opposite direction.

Direct current (d.c.) flows in one direction only, from a cell or battery. Alternating current (a.c.) reverses direction periodically, as in mains electricity.

E.m.f. and potential difference

The e.m.f. of a source is the electrical work done by the source in driving unit charge round a complete circuit:

$$E=\frac{W}{Q}$$

The potential difference (p.d.) between two points is the work done (energy converted) per unit charge moving between those points:

$$V=\frac{W}{Q}$$

Both are measured in volts, where $1\ \mathrm{V}=1\ \mathrm{J/C}$. The distinction matters: e.m.f. describes energy supplied to the charges, while p.d. describes energy converted by a component. A voltmeter is connected in parallel across the component.

For sources in series the e.m.f.s add; note that cells connected in opposite directions subtract.

Resistance

Resistance measures how difficult it is for current to pass through a component:

$$R=\frac{V}{I}\qquad\text{so}\qquad V=IR$$

The unit is the ohm ($\Omega$). To measure the resistance of a component, connect an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel, take several pairs of readings, and calculate $R=\dfrac{V}{I}$ for each.

The resistance of a wire increases when it is longer, thinner, hotter, or made of a material with a higher resistivity.

Current–voltage characteristics

  • Metal wire at constant temperature: a straight line through the origin — current is proportional to p.d., so the resistance is constant. This is Ohm's law.
  • Filament lamp: the graph curves because the filament heats up and its resistance rises, so the current increases less than proportionally.
  • Semiconductor diode: it conducts readily in one direction (forward bias) above about $0.6\ \mathrm{V}$, but almost not at all in the reverse direction.

Electrical power and energy

Power is the rate of energy transfer:

$$P=VI$$

and the energy transferred in time $t$ is

$$E=VIt$$

For the mains supply, energy is billed in kilowatt-hours (kW h). One kilowatt-hour is the energy used by a $1\ \mathrm{kW}$ appliance running for one hour:

$$1\ \mathrm{kW\,h}=1000\ \mathrm{W}\times 3600\ \mathrm{s}=3.6\times 10^6\ \mathrm{J}$$

Cost = number of kW h × cost per kW h.

Series circuits

In a series circuit there is only one path, so:

  • the current is the same at every point;
  • the e.m.f. is shared between the components, so the p.d.s add up to the supply e.m.f.;
  • the combined resistance is the sum of the individual resistances: $R=R_1+R_2$.

Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit each branch is connected directly across the supply, so:

  • the p.d. across each branch is the same;
  • the current from the source is the sum of the branch currents (the junction rule);
  • the combined resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance: $\dfrac{1}{R}=\dfrac{1}{R_1}+\dfrac{1}{R_2}$.

Lamps are connected in parallel in domestic wiring so that each gets the full mains p.d. and can be switched independently — if one fails, the others stay lit.

Potential dividers

Two resistors in series divide the supply p.d. between them in proportion to their resistances:

$$\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}$$

Using a variable resistor (potentiometer) as the divider gives an output p.d. that can be varied from zero up to the supply p.d. Replacing one resistor with a thermistor gives a temperature-sensitive output, and with an LDR gives a light-sensitive output — the basis of many sensor circuits.

Electrical safety

A mains circuit has three wires: the live wire (at a high alternating p.d.), the neutral wire (completed circuit, near earth p.d.) and the earth wire (a safety connection to the ground).

Main hazards:

  • Damaged insulation — the live wire may be touched, or may short to earth.
  • Overheating cables — too large a current in too thin a cable can start a fire.
  • Damp conditions — water conducts, so it greatly increases the risk of shock.

Protection devices:

  • A fuse is a thin wire that melts when the current exceeds its rating, breaking the circuit. It is fitted in the live wire.
  • A trip switch (circuit breaker) opens automatically when the current exceeds a set value, and can be reset.
  • Earthing connects the metal case of an appliance to the ground, so that if the live wire touches the case a large current flows to earth and blows the fuse rather than making the case live.

Electromagnetic induction

If a conductor moves across a magnetic field (or the field around it changes), an e.m.f. is induced across the conductor. If the circuit is complete, an induced current flows.

The magnitude of the induced e.m.f. increases when the conductor moves faster, the magnetic field is stronger, and the length of conductor in the field is greater (for example a coil with more turns). The direction of the induced current opposes the change that caused it, so more work is needed to produce more current.

The a.c. generator

A coil rotating in a magnetic field has an e.m.f. induced in it. Because the coil cuts the field at a varying rate, the induced e.m.f. alternates: it is zero when the coil is at right angles to the field and maximum when it is parallel to the field. Slip rings and brushes keep the connection to the rotating coil while allowing the current to reverse, giving an alternating output that varies sinusoidally with time.

Magnetic field of a current

Any current-carrying conductor has a magnetic field around it:

  • Around a straight wire the field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire; reversing the current reverses the field direction.
  • Around a solenoid (a long coil) the field is like that of a bar magnet, with a north and a south end.

The field is stronger with a larger current, more turns per unit length, and (in a solenoid) an iron core. This is the basis of the electromagnet.

The motor effect

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force, provided the conductor is not parallel to the field. The force is greatest when the conductor is perpendicular to the field and zero when it is parallel to it.

The three directions are mutually perpendicular and are given by Fleming's left-hand rule:

  • First finger — direction of the magnetic Field (north to south)
  • Second finger — direction of the Current
  • Thumb — direction of the Thrust (force, i.e. motion)

Reversing either the current or the field reverses the force; reversing both leaves it unchanged.

The d.c. motor

A rectangular coil carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a turning effect because the force on one side of the coil is upwards while the force on the other side is downwards. A commutator (a split ring) reverses the current each half-turn, so the turning effect always acts in the same direction. The turning effect is increased by more turns, a larger current and a stronger field.

Transformers

A transformer consists of a primary coil and a secondary coil wound on a soft iron core. An alternating current in the primary produces a changing magnetic field, which is carried by the core to the secondary, where it induces an alternating e.m.f. Transformers only work with a.c.

$$\frac{V_p}{V_s}=\frac{N_p}{N_s}$$

A step-up transformer has more turns on the secondary and increases the voltage; a step-down transformer has fewer turns and decreases it.

For high-voltage transmission, electricity is stepped up to a very high voltage and correspondingly low current. Because the power lost as heat in the cables is $I^2R$, reducing the current dramatically reduces the losses.

Assuming 100% efficiency, the input power equals the output power:

$$V_p I_p=V_s I_s$$

Nuclear Physics

Notes

The nuclear model of the atom

An atom consists of a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing most of the mass, surrounded by electrons in orbit. The atom as a whole is electrically neutral because the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

An atom becomes an ion when it loses or gains electrons: losing electrons leaves a positive ion, gaining electrons gives a negative ion. Ionisation never changes the element, because the number of protons is unchanged.

The alpha particle scattering experiment

In the Geiger–Marsden experiment, alpha particles were fired at a very thin gold foil. The observations were:

  1. Most alpha particles passed straight through with no deflection.
  2. A few were deflected through small angles.
  3. A very small number (about 1 in 8000) bounced back through more than $90^\circ$.

The conclusions:

  • Most of the atom is empty space (most particles are undeflected).
  • The atom contains a small, dense, positively charged nucleus — positive, because it repels the positive alpha particles, and dense because a few are turned right back.
  • The electrons orbit far outside the nucleus.

This experiment overturned the earlier "plum pudding" model, in which the positive charge was spread throughout the atom.

Nuclide notation and isotopes

The proton number $Z$ is the number of protons; it identifies the element. The nucleon number $A$ is the total number of protons plus neutrons. Therefore:

$$\text{number of neutrons}=A-Z$$

A nuclide is written as ${}^{A}_{Z}X$, where $X$ is the chemical symbol. For example, ${}^{235}_{\ 92}\mathrm{U}$ has 92 protons and $235-92=143$ neutrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same $Z$) with different numbers of neutrons (different $A$). They have identical chemical properties, because chemistry depends on the electrons, but their nuclei differ in stability — some isotopes are stable, others radioactive.

Nuclear fission and fusion

Fission is the splitting of a large, unstable nucleus (such as uranium-235) into two smaller nuclei plus neutrons, releasing energy. If the released neutrons go on to split further nuclei, a chain reaction results; this is controlled in a nuclear power station.

Fusion is the joining of two small nuclei (such as isotopes of hydrogen) to form a larger nucleus, releasing energy. Fusion is the process that powers the Sun and other stars, and it requires extremely high temperatures and pressures.

Background radiation

Background radiation is the low-level ionising radiation that is always present. Significant sources include:

  • radon gas in the air (from rocks and soil) — usually the largest natural contribution;
  • rocks and soil, and building materials made from them;
  • cosmic rays from space (more at high altitude);
  • food and drink (naturally occurring radioactive isotopes);
  • medical and industrial uses (a small artificial contribution).

Because background radiation is always present, any measurement of a source's activity must be corrected: count the background separately, then subtract it from the count measured with the source present.

$$\text{corrected count rate}=\text{measured count rate}-\text{background count rate}$$

Counts should be taken over a reasonably long time and averaged, because radioactive decay is random.

Detecting radiation

  • Photographic film — radiation darkens the film; film badges are worn by workers to monitor their dose.
  • Geiger–Müller (GM) tube with a counter — each detected particle produces a pulse; the counter gives a count rate in counts per second or per minute. A GM tube detects alpha particles only if they can enter the tube, so it has a thin mica window.

In electric and magnetic fields, alpha and beta are deflected in opposite directions because their charges are opposite, and beta is deflected much more because an electron's mass is far smaller than an alpha particle's. Gamma is not deflected because it is uncharged.

Radioactive decay and decay equations

Radioactive decay is a change in an unstable nucleus; it is random and spontaneous, and it is unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemical combination. When a nucleus emits alpha or beta radiation it becomes a nucleus of a different element.

The rules for balancing a decay equation:

  • Alpha decay: $A$ decreases by 4 and $Z$ decreases by 2. An alpha particle is ${}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$.
  • Beta decay: $A$ is unchanged and $Z$ increases by 1 (a neutron turns into a proton plus an electron, and the electron is ejected). Write the beta particle as ${}^{\ 0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$.
  • Gamma emission: neither $A$ nor $Z$ changes; the nucleus simply loses surplus energy.

Two examples:

$${}^{238}_{\ 92}\mathrm{U}\ \rightarrow\ {}^{234}_{\ 90}\mathrm{Th}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$$

$${}^{14}_{\ 6}\mathrm{C}\ \rightarrow\ {}^{14}_{\ 7}\mathrm{N}+{}^{\ 0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$$

In every equation the total nucleon number and the total proton number must be the same on both sides.

Half-life

The half-life of an isotope is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei (and hence the count rate) to fall to half its initial value.

Half-life is constant for a given isotope — it does not depend on how many nuclei are present, and it cannot be changed. After $n$ half-lives the fraction remaining is $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$.

To find the half-life from a decay curve, choose a starting count rate, halve it, read off the time at which the curve reaches that halved value, and subtract the starting time.

Choosing a source and working safely

The choice of source depends on the job:

  • A tracer in the body should be a gamma emitter (so it can escape the body to be detected) with a half-life long enough to do the scan but short enough to decay quickly afterwards.
  • A thickness gauge for paper uses a beta source, because the paper partially absorbs beta and the change in count rate reveals the thickness.
  • A smoke detector uses an alpha source, because alpha ionises the air strongly and smoke absorbs the alpha particles, changing the current.

Ionising radiation can damage living cells, causing mutations, cell death, radiation burns and an increased risk of cancer. Safety precautions:

  • keep the source as far away as possible and use handling tools (tongs);
  • minimise the time of exposure;
  • use shielding (lead screens, or perspex for beta);
  • store sources in a clearly labelled, lead-lined container when not in use;
  • wear a film badge to monitor the accumulated dose;
  • never point a source at anyone, and never touch or ingest it.

Examples (3)

Example 1

A radioactive source has an initial count rate of $800$ counts/minute. After $12$ hours the count rate is $100$ counts/minute. Background radiation is $20$ counts/minute. Calculate the half-life of the source.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

First correct for background:

  • initial corrected count rate $=800-20=780$ counts/minute;
  • final corrected count rate $=100-20=80$ counts/minute.

Now find how many halvings take $780$ down to $80$: $780\to 390\to 195\to 97.5\to 48.75$. After three half-lives the count rate is $97.5$, and after four it is $48.75$; $80$ lies between these, close to three half-lives.

A cleaner approach: $780\times\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}=80$, so $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}=0.103$, giving $n\approx 3.3$ half-lives in $12$ hours.

$$\text{half-life}\approx\frac{12}{3.3}\approx 3.6\ \text{hours}$$

Example 2

Radon-222 decays by alpha emission. Write the balanced decay equation and identify the daughter nuclide.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

Radon has $Z=86$. In alpha decay $A$ falls by 4 and $Z$ falls by 2:

$${}^{222}_{\ 86}\mathrm{Rn}\ \rightarrow\ {}^{218}_{\ 84}\mathrm{Po}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$$

Check: $222=218+4$ and $86=84+2$. The daughter nuclide has $Z=84$, which is polonium, so it is polonium-218.

Example 3

In the alpha particle scattering experiment, most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil but a very small number were deflected through more than $90^\circ$. Explain what each observation shows.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

Most passing straight through shows that the atom is mostly empty space — the alpha particles rarely come close enough to anything to be deflected.

The few large-angle deflections show that the positive charge and nearly all the mass of the atom are concentrated in a very small, dense nucleus. Only an alpha particle heading almost directly at such a nucleus experiences a large enough repulsive force to be turned back; since the nucleus is tiny, this happens very rarely.

Notes

The Earth and the Moon

The Earth is a planet. It:

  • rotates on its own axis once approximately every 24 hours, which produces day and night;
  • orbits the Sun once approximately every 365 days (one year).

The Earth's axis is tilted, and combined with the yearly orbit this tilt produces the seasons: when a hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun it receives more direct radiation and has longer days, so it is summer there.

The Moon is a natural satellite. It orbits the Earth approximately once every month (about 27.3 days for one orbit; about 29.5 days between identical phases). The changing relative positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon produce the phases of the Moon, and the alignment of all three produces eclipses.

The Solar System

The Solar System consists of:

  • one star, the Sun, at the centre, containing most of the mass of the Solar System;
  • eight planets orbiting the Sun, in order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune;
  • minor planets — dwarf planets such as Pluto, and asteroids, mostly in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter;
  • comets — lumps of ice and dust in highly elliptical orbits;
  • moons (natural satellites) orbiting the planets.

The four inner planets are small and rocky, while the four outer planets are much larger and are gas giants. Jupiter is by far the largest planet.

Gravitational field strength and orbits

Every mass attracts every other mass. The gravitational field strength $g$ at a point is the force per unit mass placed there; it depends on the mass of the body producing the field and on the distance from it — a more massive planet has a stronger field, and the field gets weaker further away.

The gravitational force is what keeps a planet in orbit around the Sun and a moon in orbit around a planet. The force acts towards the centre of the orbit, continuously changing the direction of the orbiting body's velocity, so the orbit is a continuously "falling" path.

Orbits are elliptical, not perfectly circular. In an elliptical orbit the speed is not constant: an object moves faster when it is closer to the body it orbits, because the gravitational force (and therefore the acceleration) is greater there. This is why comets race through the inner Solar System and crawl at the outer edge of their orbits.

Orbital speed

For an approximately circular orbit of radius $r$, the distance travelled in one orbit is the circumference $2\pi r$, and the time taken is the period $T$, so the average orbital speed is

$$v=\frac{2\pi r}{T}$$

This works for any orbiting body — a planet round the Sun, the Moon round the Earth, or a satellite round the Earth — provided you use the radius of the orbit, not the radius of the body.

The Sun and other stars

The Sun is a star of medium size. It is powered by nuclear fusion, in which hydrogen nuclei join to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy. The Sun contains most of the mass of the Solar System, which is why everything else orbits it.

A light-year is the distance light travels in one year:

$$1\ \text{light-year}=9.5\times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{m}$$

Light travels at $3.0\times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in a vacuum, so light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach the Earth, while light from the next nearest star takes over four years. Looking out into space is therefore looking back in time.

The life cycle of a star

A star begins as a cloud of dust and gas called a nebula. Gravity pulls the material together; as it collapses it heats up, and when the core becomes hot and dense enough, nuclear fusion begins and a star is born. The star then spends most of its life as a main sequence star, in which the inward pull of gravity is balanced by the outward pressure from the energy released by fusion — a stable balance that lasts billions of years.

What happens next depends on the mass of the star:

  • A star like the Sun eventually expands into a red giant, then sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a small, hot, dense white dwarf behind, which slowly cools and fades.
  • A much more massive star becomes a red supergiant, then explodes as a supernova. The remnant is either an extremely dense neutron star or, if massive enough, a black hole, whose gravity is so strong that not even light can escape.

Galaxies and the Universe

The Milky Way is a galaxy — a huge collection of many billions of stars held together by gravity — and our Sun is one of those stars. The Milky Way is one of many billions of galaxies in the Universe.

Redshift and the expanding Universe

When a source of light moves away from an observer, the observed wavelength of its light is increased — the light is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum. This is redshift.

Observations show that the light from distant galaxies is redshifted, and that the redshift is greater for more distant galaxies. The conclusion is that distant galaxies are moving away from us, and that the further away they are, the faster they are receding: the Universe is expanding.

Note that it is the space between galaxies that is expanding; the galaxies themselves are being carried apart rather than flying through space from a single point.

Hubble's law

The speed of recession $v$ of a galaxy is proportional to its distance $d$ from us:

$$v=H_0 d$$

$H_0$ is the Hubble constant, defined as the ratio of the speed of recession to the distance. Its current estimate is about $2.2\times 10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Because $\dfrac{1}{H_0}$ has the units of time, it gives a rough estimate of the age of the Universe.

In practice the distance of a far galaxy is estimated from its redshift, and then Hubble's law is used to infer how fast it is receding.

The cosmic microwave background radiation

Astronomers detect low-intensity microwave radiation arriving almost uniformly from all directions in the sky. This is the cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR).

Its existence is strong evidence for the Big Bang model. The Universe began extremely hot and dense; as it expanded it cooled, and the radiation produced shortly after the Big Bang has been stretched to microwave wavelengths by the expansion of space itself. It is, in effect, the afterglow of the early Universe, and its presence is very hard to explain in any steady-state model.

Examples (3)

Example 1

A satellite orbits the Earth at a height such that its orbital radius is $7.0\times 10^6\ \mathrm{m}$, and it completes one orbit in $5.8\times 10^3\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate its average orbital speed.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

$$v=\frac{2\pi r}{T}=\frac{2\pi\times 7.0\times 10^6}{5.8\times 10^3}=\frac{4.40\times 10^7}{5.8\times 10^3}\approx 7.6\times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

So the satellite travels at about $7.6\ \mathrm{km/s}$.

Example 2

The Sun is about $1.5\times 10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}$ from the Earth. Calculate how long light takes to travel from the Sun to the Earth, using $c=3.0\times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

$$t=\frac{d}{c}=\frac{1.5\times 10^{11}}{3.0\times 10^8}=5.0\times 10^2=500\ \mathrm{s}$$

That is about $8.3$ minutes. So we see the Sun as it was about eight minutes ago.

Example 3

A galaxy is found to be receding at $4.4\times 10^4\ \mathrm{km/s}$. Using $H_0=2.2\times 10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, estimate its distance from the Earth in metres.

Solution (click to expand/collapse)

First convert the speed to metres per second: $v=4.4\times 10^4\ \mathrm{km/s}=4.4\times 10^7\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

$$d=\frac{v}{H_0}=\frac{4.4\times 10^7}{2.2\times 10^{-18}}=2.0\times 10^{25}\ \mathrm{m}$$

So the galaxy is about $2.0\times 10^{25}\ \mathrm{m}$ away. (In light-years this is $\dfrac{2.0\times 10^{25}}{9.5\times 10^{15}}\approx 2.1\times 10^9$ light-years.)
