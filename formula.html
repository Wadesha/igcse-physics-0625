Equations Book

Cambridge IGCSE Physics (0625) gives candidates no separate formula booklet — every equation below has to be recalled and used correctly. Part 1 lists them by syllabus topic, Part 2 covers units, prefixes and constants, and Part 3 sets out the standard practical methods.

These are the quantitative relationships listed in the 0625 syllabus. Learn what each symbol means as well as the equation itself — and always check that your values are in the matching SI units before substituting.

3 · Practical methods

These are procedures rather than single formulae — learn the steps, the apparatus and the quantities you measure.

Density of an irregular solid

  1. Measure the mass of the object with a balance.
  2. Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water and record the volume.
  3. Lower the object in until fully submerged and record the new volume; the difference is the volume of the object.
  4. Calculate the density from mass divided by volume.

For a regular solid, measure its dimensions instead and calculate the volume geometrically.

Specific heat capacity of a metal block

  1. Weigh the block and record its mass.
  2. Place a heater in the hole in the block, with a thermometer alongside, and insulate the block.
  3. Record the initial temperature, switch on for a measured time, and record the final temperature.
  4. Measure the energy supplied with a joulemeter, or from the voltage, current and time.
  5. Calculate the specific heat capacity from the energy, the mass and the temperature rise.

Resistance of a component

  1. Connect the component in series with an ammeter, a battery and a variable resistor.
  2. Connect a voltmeter in parallel across the component.
  3. Vary the variable resistor to obtain several pairs of current and p.d. readings.
  4. Calculate the resistance from each pair and average the results.

Speed of sound in air (echo method)

  1. Stand a large measured distance from a tall wall or cliff.
  2. Clap and start timing; stop when you hear the echo.
  3. Repeat for a number of claps and divide to obtain the time for one echo.
  4. Double the distance to the wall, then divide by the echo time to get the speed.

Refractive index of a glass block

  1. Place a rectangular block on paper and draw round it.
  2. Shine a narrow ray into the block; mark the incident and emergent rays.
  3. Remove the block and draw the ray inside it; draw the normal at the point of entry.
  4. Measure the angle of incidence and the angle of refraction, and calculate their sines.
  5. Calculate the refractive index as the ratio of the two sines.

Half-life from a decay curve

  1. Measure the background count rate with no source present, and subtract it from every reading.
  2. Plot the corrected count rate against time.
  3. Take any starting count rate and halve it; read off the time at which the curve reaches that value.
  4. The difference between the two times is the half-life; repeat at another point to check.

Radiation safety

  • Handle sources with tongs, never with bare hands, and never point a source at anyone.
  • Keep sources in a labelled, lead-lined container when not in use.
  • Minimise the time of exposure and maximise the distance from the source.
  • Use shielding: perspex for beta, lead for gamma; alpha is stopped by paper but must never be ingested.
  • Wear a film badge so that the accumulated dose is monitored.
